# Chapter 66: Ledger

The report reached Roderic before dawn, carried up through the keep by a rider who had not slept and did not try to hide it. He read it once, standing, the candle at his elbow burned down to little more than a stub, and found that the words did not need a second reading to settle.

*The King's dragon left Aurelia in the grey hour before morning. Alone. North.*

He set the paper down with the same care he gave anything that mattered, and did not allow himself the satisfaction of being surprised.

Aldric Kaelarion had not flown anywhere in years. A king who left his own capital left it for exactly one reason serious enough to justify the risk of being seen doing it, and Roderic had spent long enough studying the man across a decade of careful, bloodless correspondence to know he would not choose the sky over the road unless the road had already become too slow to matter. Not a diplomatic errand. Not a family visit dressed up as one. A king moving on something he trusted to no one but himself and a dragon did not do so lightly, and he had not raised an army the size of the one now quartered across his own lands without understanding, clearly, what that kind of urgency meant aimed in his direction.

His hand had been shown. Not proven — Aldric would not have moved on rumor, and Roderic had built the last several years on the assumption that nothing conclusive existed to find — but shown enough that the man himself had judged it worth a king's own dragon and a king's own risk to carry it north in person.

Which meant the real question was not whether Caldarion knew. It was how.

He did not have to wait long for the second report, and some part of him — the part that had spent thirty years learning not to trust comfort of any kind — understood, reading the first line, that he had known the shape of this answer for longer than he had let himself examine it.

Harwick delivered it himself rather than send it up by a lesser hand, which told Roderic most of what he needed to know about how bad it was before the man had finished the first sentence. Someone within the house had been passing word out — not documents, nothing so clumsy as a stolen ledger, but detail. Which of the allied houses had been asked to send men. How long the asking had been going on. The exact, careful specificity of a question only someone who already knew where to look would think to ask.

"Maren," Roderic said, before Harwick had finished laying it out, and did not make it a question.

Harwick did not correct him. "She has not been seen at the keep in six days, my lord. Her rooms were emptied with more care than panic usually allows for. I do not believe this was discovered and fled. I believe she was already gone before we thought to look."

Roderic absorbed that the way he absorbed most things that could not be undone by absorbing them harder. He did not raise his voice. He did not, as some distant and useless part of him almost wanted to, ask Harwick to say it again, as though a second telling might reveal it to have been some other cousin, some more forgivable name. Maren had sat at his own table more times than he could count. Maren had never once, in all the years he had known her, given him cause to watch her the way he watched everyone else who came near anything that mattered.

That, he understood now, coldly and without much room left for anger, had been the entire point.

"Isolde," he said next, and this time it was not even a question worth the shape of one.

"There is no proof that reaches her directly, my lord. Only the shape of where the questions led."

"There does not need to be proof." Roderic moved to the window, the same one his father had stood at before him, looking out over land that had belonged to this house since long before either Kaelarion or Caldarion existed to dispute it. "There needs to be a door the questions could only have come through, and I have known which door that was for longer than I let myself say it aloud." He had suspected her for years — not with evidence, only with the particular certainty of a man who had watched his sister leave for a wedding she did not want and had never once, in the two decades since, heard her speak of this house the way a person spoke of something they still belonged to. He had chosen, for reasons he was no longer entirely sure did him credit, not to look closely enough to confirm it. Looking closely would have cost him a sister he had not yet finished deciding whether he still had.

He did not have that luxury any longer.

"What of the rest of it," he said. "Beyond Maren."

"An old family retainer. One of the ones nobody thinks twice about because they have simply always been there, longer than most of the household can remember them not being. We are questioning him now." Harwick's voice did not change, but something in the way he said it told Roderic the questioning would not be gentle, and Roderic found he had no particular objection to that. A man who had spent thirty years in this house and repaid it by carrying its business out the back door did not merit the same restraint he was, for now, still extending to his own blood.

"Find Maren," he said. "Quietly. I want her found before I want her punished — the two are not the same task, and I would rather have the first done well than the second done quickly." He did not say what he intended once she was found, mostly because he had not yet decided, and he did not waste words on decisions he had not finished making.

He turned the two reports over together in his mind, the way he had learned to turn over anything that arrived close enough in time to matter as a pair rather than as two separate facts. Aldric knew. Aldric had known enough, for long enough, to build a case worth flying north over — which meant the buildup Roderic had spent years assembling, patiently, past the point where he could have stopped even if some clearer-headed version of himself had wanted to, was no longer a secret held at arm's length by careful deniability. It was a fact sitting on a table in Blackspire, in front of the one man in the kingdom whose army could actually meet his own without flinching.

The clever calculation would have been to wait. Let the last pieces finish arriving — the northern levies not yet fully mustered, the grain stores not yet at the number he had promised himself before he would call this ready. Wait, and let Caldarion spend the coming weeks bracing for a blow that had not yet decided when it was coming, wearing down its own readiness against a clock only Roderic controlled.

That calculation had assumed he still controlled the clock.

He no longer did. Aldric's dragon in the sky over the North had taken that from him as surely as if he had announced it himself, and there was no version of waiting now that did not simply hand Caldarion the one thing Roderic could still, barely, deny them — the choice of when this actually began. A war fought on Vesper Caldarion's own timing, prepared and rested and warned, was a war Roderic did not like his odds in nearly as well as the one he had spent years quietly building toward. A war fought before that preparation finished settling into place was still, by his own accounting, a war he could win.

He would rather move too soon than move exactly when his enemy expected him to.

"Send word to the western levies," he said, and found, saying it, that the cold settled somewhere beneath the anger rather than replacing it, which was its own kind of relief — he had never much trusted men who could not tell the two apart. "We march within the week. Firsthold first, and everything Caldarion has to spend keeping it fixed there while it happens."

Harwick did not ask what "it" meant. He had served long enough to know when a question would only cost him an answer he was better off not carrying alone.

"And Maren?"

"When she is found," Roderic said, "she is found. Everything else waits for the war to decide it." He did not look away from the window as he said it, out over land his father had bled for and never reclaimed, and thought, with the particular grim clarity of a man who had finally stopped pretending the choice was still ahead of him rather than already made, that whatever else this cost him, it would not be the mistake of hesitating twice at the same door.

Korrath stirred somewhere below in the roost, restless with a season's worth of readiness that had, until this morning, still technically been a choice. Roderic went down to see him before the sun had fully cleared the eastern wall, and found, laying a hand against scales that had outlasted one war already and were about to be asked to survive a second, that he did not feel the fear he had half expected to. Only the old, familiar weight of a grievance finally given somewhere to go.
