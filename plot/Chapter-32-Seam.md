# Chapter 32: Seam

"Both of us," Riven said, "at once."

They were in the residence's training yard, mid-morning, the sand still cool from the night before, and Lucian looked between his brothers with the particular flat patience he reserved for proposals that were technically questions but had already been decided before they were asked.

"You have sparred me together before."

"Not properly." Thorne said it quieter than Riven had, but with no less certainty behind it. "Dain keeps us apart when he drills you against the two of us. Half measures, so neither of you gets hurt learning something before you're ready for it. We are asking for the whole measure."

"Your father is inside."

"Our father," Riven said, "told us this morning that Aurelia has made us restless, and that restlessness untested becomes carelessness. I intend to prove him wrong about the second part." A grin, quick and entirely without malice. "You are the only test worth the name."

Lucian considered them both a moment longer — Riven already loosening his shoulders with the barely contained energy he brought to everything physical, Thorne standing still and watchful a pace behind him, weight already settled the way a fighter settles it before a match rather than a boy settles it before a game — and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he wanted this as much as they did.

"Two falls," he said. "Not to injury. If either of you loses your temper, it ends."

"It will not need to end," Riven said, and Thorne, beside him, said nothing at all, which was answer enough in its own right.

They came at him together, and within the first exchange Lucian understood why Dain had kept them apart this long. Separately, each brother was formidable in the plain, uncomplicated way youth and talent produced without needing to be clever about it — Riven all forward pressure, strikes thrown with a conviction that dared the world to prove him wrong about where they'd land; Thorne quieter, more exact, reading distance and angle with a patience that outstripped his brother's by a wide margin. Together, they were something else entirely. Riven drove in first, forcing Lucian to commit to a defense, to spend the attention a defense required — and in the half-breath that attention cost him, Thorne was already moving into the space Riven's pressure had opened, not following his brother's lead so much as completing a shape the two of them had clearly built between themselves across years of training Lucian had only ever watched from the outside.

He gave ground. Not from necessity — not yet — but because giving ground taught him the rhythm faster than standing his ground would have, and the rhythm, once he had it, was worth more than any single exchange. Riven's aggression bought Thorne his openings. Thorne's precision covered the gaps Riven's forward momentum left undefended. Where either brother alone would have been readable within a handful of exchanges, together they closed on him from two directions at once, each covering exactly what the other lacked, and for the first time since Firsthold, Lucian felt something in a spar that was not quite the cold, familiar arithmetic of an opponent being solved.

It was, he understood even as he retreated another half-step under the combined weight of it, actually difficult.

The Still Blade did not ask him to match their aggression or their precision on its own terms. It asked him to control what neither of them had learned yet to control themselves — distance, timing, the small compounding cost of every exchange that two attackers, however well matched to each other, paid twice over for every mistake a single opponent paid for once. He let Riven overextend on the fourth exchange, not by luck but by design, and used the half-second Riven needed to recover to close the distance Thorne had been patiently trying to hold open. Thorne adjusted fast — faster than Lucian had expected, fast enough that the boy's instincts alone very nearly closed the opening again before Lucian's blade found the flat of his shoulder in a strike that would have ended a real fight cleanly.

"First fall," Lucian said.

Riven swore, more impressed than frustrated, and came back in without waiting for Thorne to reset, which was itself the difference between them laid bare in a single choice — Riven trusting the attack to fix what the attack had already cost them, Thorne, recovering more slowly, trying instead to rebuild the shape of the thing they had lost. For a while it worked. The second fall took longer than the first, Lucian giving more ground than he'd needed to give in the opening exchange, genuinely tested by a coordination that adapted faster than either brother's individual skill should have allowed. But the gap that had decided the first fall decided the second as well — not talent, which the twins had in true and startling abundance, but the years Lucian had spent learning to make an opponent's strength arrive a half-beat too late to matter. He turned Thorne's guarded patience into an opening by refusing to give Riven the reckless exchange Riven wanted, forcing the fight to slow into the exact tempo Thorne preferred and Riven did not, and in the moment that mismatch cracked their rhythm apart, Lucian ended it.

Both brothers went down onto the sand within a breath of each other, and lay there a moment, chests heaving, before Riven started laughing — short, disbelieving, entirely without bitterness in it.

"Two falls," Lucian said, offering a hand down to each of them in turn. "You made me work for both."

"You are older," Thorne said, accepting the hand, steadier on his feet than his brother despite having taken the harder fall. "And you have had Dain since before either of us could hold a blade properly."

"That is not nothing. It is also not the whole of it." Lucian meant it, and found, saying it, that he wanted them to hear it plainly rather than as consolation. "Alone, either of you is dangerous. Together you are something Dain has clearly been keeping from becoming a problem for me specifically, and I understand now why he waited this long to let it happen."

Riven's grin came back at that, wide and entirely unguarded. "Then we fight you together again. Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Lucian agreed.

They parted at the yard's edge with the loose, easy energy of a morning well spent, and it was there, brushing sand from his sleeves, that Lucian watched the two of them come apart in a smaller, quieter way than the fight itself had shown. Riven was already moving toward the residence's outer wall, restless with the fight still in his blood, calling back over his shoulder that he meant to find the Silver Guard's training master and see whether Aurelia's own soldiers were worth the name. Thorne did not follow him.

"You are not going with him," Lucian said. It was not quite a question.

"He wants another fight. I have had enough fighting for one morning." Thorne looked past Lucian, toward the walled yard beyond the residence where Ancalion's dark shape lay half-visible over the rooftop, wings folded, still. "I think I will go sit with your dragon instead, if he will allow it. He is quieter than most things in this city. I find I want quiet more than I want another bout today."

"He does not usually allow strangers close."

"I am not asking to be close. I am asking to be nearby." Something in Thorne's expression, tired and unguarded in a way the sparring had not made him, settled into a small, private certainty. "There is a difference, I think, between wanting to be let in and wanting simply to sit where something large and old and unhurried is breathing. I have wanted the second more than the first, most of my life. Riven has never understood that. I do not expect him to."

Lucian watched him go, unhurried, toward the walled yard and the watchful black shape waiting in it, and understood, without needing the shape of it fully explained to him, that he had just watched two brothers who fought as a single seamless thing come apart, cleanly and without complaint, into two people who had simply never been the same person wearing two faces to begin with.
