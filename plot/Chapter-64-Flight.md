# Chapter 64: Flight

The evidence lay across the table in the order Aldric had arranged it three times now, as though a fourth arrangement might make it easier to look at than the first. It did not. The Corvane coin-trail, painstaking and years in the assembling. The Crown's Eyes' latest accounting of ships and grain and iron moving through Dravenhall's ports in quantities no honest household required. And beneath both, in Isolde's own careful hand, the confirmation he had least wanted and most needed — figures she could only have gathered by asking questions inside her own family that no dutiful daughter should have had cause to ask.

He had suspected for years. Suspicion, it turned out, was a far gentler thing to carry than certainty. Suspicion left room for being wrong.

There was no room left now. Roderic Dravenhall had spent the better part of a decade building, quietly and patiently, the largest army on the continent by raw numbers alone — not the finest, not the best equipped, but large enough that its purpose could no longer be mistaken for caution. A man did not build an army that size to leave it standing idle indefinitely. Aldric had sat with enough generals and enough ledgers in twenty-three years on the throne to know a readied force from a defensive one, and what Isolde's figures described was the former.

The only true uncertainty left was when. And uncertainty of that particular kind, Aldric had learned young, was not a thing that improved with more time spent studying it.

He could send a letter. He turned the thought over for the hundredth time and set it down again for the same reasons as before. A sealed message, however carefully worded, however trusted the rider, was a single thread — one horse, one road, one set of hands it had to pass through unbroken the entire distance north. Dravenhall had spies enough inside this capital that Aldric no longer had the comfort of assuming any road was truly private. A letter could be lost. A letter could be read. A letter could arrive a full season after whatever it warned against had already happened, and no one would ever be entirely certain which.

A king flying his own dragon north was not subtle. That, he had finally understood sometime in the last restless week, was precisely its use. If Dravenhall's people saw Solara leave the capital, they would not need to intercept a single word to understand that something urgent moved toward Blackspire, only that it moved, and moved from him personally. Roderic would have to decide, quickly, whether that meant his hand had already been shown. A patient man forced to assume he had lost the advantage of surprise made different choices than a patient man still convinced he held it. Aldric could not stop Dravenhall from choosing its own moment. He could, perhaps, take that moment away from Roderic and hand him a worse one instead — force him to move now, on ground where Vesper had already been warned and was already bracing, rather than months from now on whatever fully prepared morning Roderic himself would have chosen.

It was not a comfortable calculation. It was, as far as he could see, the only one that did not simply hand Dravenhall the advantage of time along with everything else.

And it was not, he reminded himself, a wager made from nothing. He and Vesper had spent years without proof doing precisely what men without proof were supposed to do and rarely did — preparing quietly, at cost, against a thing neither of them could yet name aloud to anyone else. Aldric had spent that time routing the Crown's Eyes' attention where his instincts, not his evidence, told him to look, had positioned certain alliances a little more carefully than their surface reasons required. Vesper, he knew only in shape and not in detail, had done a version of the same on his own side of the mountains — a readiness Aldric had never once asked to see the particulars of, because a king who demanded to inspect his most powerful ally's private preparations was not truly treating him as an ally at all. Better prepared and not need it than the reverse. It was as close to a shared creed as the two of them had ever spoken plainly to one another, on the one night decades ago when Vesper had told him, quietly and without any legal obligation to, that Morvath had survived the war after all. Aldric was flying north now on the strength of that same old trust, not blind to what he was asking, but not asking it of a man starting from nothing either.

Isolde was the part of the calculation he could not make sit easily, however many times he turned it. She had given him the figures that confirmed her own family was very possibly weeks or months from open war, had done it without flinching, without once asking him to soften what he did with the knowledge. He believed, as he had believed for over twenty years now, that her loyalty to this crown and to their children was real, was not some deeper performance laid over her father's ambitions. But belief was not proof, and there were people close enough to her — attendants, cousins, old family retainers she had never thought to question because she had known some of them since girlhood — who did not need her knowledge or her permission to carry word of what moved through this palace. He would not tell her he was flying north. Not because he doubted her. Because he could not risk what might be taken from her without her ever knowing it had been taken at all.

He went to see Solara himself rather than send for her.

She met him in the roost with the low, considering stillness she had always carried when she sensed something beyond routine in him, gold scales dulled slightly with disuse in a way that shamed him more than he expected it to. He had not flown her — truly flown her, distance and purpose rather than the short ceremonial circuits the court still occasionally required — in longer than he cared to count precisely. A king did not leave the capital. It was one of the first lessons of the crown, absorbed so early he had stopped noticing he carried it. His body remembered the motions of mounting her, the particular balance of weight and grip, in the dim, half-forgotten way a man remembers a language he learned as a child and rarely speaks. He pressed a hand to her neck and felt her breathe, slow and enormous beneath his palm, and thought, not for the first time, that this was very possibly the last uncomplicated thing left to him — a creature who required nothing of him but his presence, no calculation, no performance, only the plain fact of being there.

The rest of the arrangements took the better part of a day. He told the court he traveled north on a private matter concerning House Kaelarion's own holdings, true enough in its bones to survive scrutiny without inviting any. Dorian would hold the capital in his absence — an arrangement that cost Aldric a measure of private unease even now, after everything, though he told himself firmly that a few weeks of real responsibility, unsupervised, might do his elder son more good than another decade of careful correction ever had. Corvin found him in the roost as the preparations finished, quiet in the way Corvin always was, asking fewer questions than Dorian would have and accepting the answers Aldric gave him without pressing for the ones beneath them.

"Come back," Corvin said simply, before he left, and Aldric found it steadied him more than anything else said to him that day.

He launched from the capital in the grey hour before true morning, when the sky still held enough dark to blur detail but not enough to hide the shape of a golden dragon rising from the royal roost entirely. He felt the city's eyes on him as Solara climbed — not many, not yet, only the handful awake enough at that hour to notice and wonder, but enough. He had wanted that. Had built the entire wager on precisely that. Somewhere below, word would already be moving through channels he could not see and did not control, carried by people loyal to a house that was not his own, and there was nothing left to do about that now except let it move and trust that he had judged its shape correctly.

The mountains rose slowly ahead of him as the light came up properly, Blackspire's peak catching the first true sun long before the lowlands beneath it did, and Aldric found, climbing toward it, that whatever fear he had carried through the last sleepless week had settled, for now, into something steadier. He did not know exactly what waited for him at the end of this flight, or what it would cost either house before this was finished. He knew only that he had chosen to move first, on his own terms, rather than wait any longer to be moved against — and that whatever else came of it, Vesper Caldarion would hear this from him directly, the way it had always been between them, and would not be finding out alone.
