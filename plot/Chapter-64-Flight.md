# Chapter 64: Flight

The last piece arrived after midnight, carried up to his private study by a Crown's Eyes courier too well trained to show what the ride north through the palace's sleeping corridors had cost him. Aldric read it once, then again, and found that a second reading did not soften a single line of it. Ships. Iron. Grain moving through Dravenhall's ports in quantities no honest household required, cross-checked now against the figures Isolde had gathered without ever asking him plainly why he wanted them. He had suspected for years. Suspicion, it turned out, was a far gentler thing to carry than certainty. Suspicion left room for being wrong.

There was no room left now.

He did not sleep. He sat with the papers until the grey hour before dawn, and when he rose it was not toward his own bed but toward his daughter's rooms, on the theory — correct, as it happened — that Seren kept the same restless hours he did whenever something in the palace felt wrong to her even without being told why.

She was awake, curled into the window seat with a shawl around her shoulders and a book she had plainly stopped reading some while ago, and she set it aside the moment she saw his face.

"You look the way you looked the night before you told me about the betrothal properly, instead of letting me hear it from someone else first," she said. "What is it."

"Something I cannot tell you the whole of yet, and something I need your help with regardless." He sat across from her, choosing the words with the same care he gave a council he did not fully trust. "I need to send word north. Not through any formal channel — those are watched, more closely than I am comfortable admitting to you. I need Vesper Caldarion to understand, without anyone else understanding it alongside him, that I am coming to Blackspire myself, soon, over something too serious to wait for the ordinary post."

Seren was quiet a moment, turning that over with the same quickness she had always had, the one people mistook for guilelessness because she wore it so openly. "You want me to write to Lucian."

"I want you to write to Lucian the way you always write to Lucian. Nothing invented, nothing performed — simply true, the way your letters have always been. Somewhere inside the ordinary shape of it, tell him I have been talking about wanting to see the North again, properly, for the first time in years. Nothing more specific than that. Let Vesper do the rest of the reading himself; he will not need it explained to him twice."

"And if he asks me why, later, when you actually arrive."

"Then tell him the truth — that your father asked you to carry something he could not risk carrying any other way, and trusted you to do it without needing to understand all of it first." He reached over and closed his hand briefly around hers, more grateful than he entirely knew how to say for the plain, unhesitating way she nodded rather than pressed him further. "I would not ask this of you if it were smaller than it is."

"I know." She studied him a moment longer, the sharpness in her settling into something steadier. "I will write it tonight. I always write to him at night, when the rest of the palace has stopped needing me for anything."

He left her already reaching for paper, and did not look back to see what she wrote, because he trusted her to write it exactly as she had said she would — true, and careful, and entirely her own.

Four days later, he judged the letter had done its work. Long enough to have crossed the distance and been read; not so long that whatever window remained to him had closed while he waited on his own caution. He spent those four days finishing what a letter could never safely carry — assembling the coin-trail, the shipping figures, Isolde's own quiet contribution, into something that would hold together the moment he said it aloud, in person, to the one man in the kingdom he trusted to hear it without needing to be convinced twice.

He had already sent what warning a letter could carry. It could not carry the rest. A sealed message, however carefully worded, however trusted the rider, was a single thread — one horse, one road, one set of hands it had to pass through unbroken the entire distance north. Dravenhall had spies enough inside this capital that Aldric no longer had the comfort of assuming any road was truly private. A letter could be lost. A letter could be read. A letter could arrive a full season after whatever it warned against had already happened, and no one would ever be entirely certain which.

A king flying his own dragon north was not subtle. That, he had finally understood sometime in the last restless week, was precisely its use. If Dravenhall's people saw Solara leave the capital, they would not need to intercept a single word to understand that something urgent moved toward Blackspire, only that it moved, and moved from him personally. Roderic would have to decide, quickly, whether that meant his hand had already been shown. A patient man forced to assume he had lost the advantage of surprise made different choices than a patient man still convinced he held it. Aldric could not stop Dravenhall from choosing its own moment. He could, perhaps, take that moment away from Roderic and hand him a worse one instead — and because the letter had already done its quiet work days before, Blackspire would not be starting that clock from nothing either. Vesper had been warned once, gently. He was about to be warned again, unmistakably, and the days already spent between the two warnings were themselves the whole of what Aldric had to offer him by way of a head start.

It was not a comfortable calculation. It was, as far as he could see, the only one that did not simply hand Dravenhall the advantage of time along with everything else.

And it was not a wager made from nothing. He and Vesper had spent years without proof doing precisely what men without proof were supposed to do and rarely did — preparing quietly, at cost, against a thing neither of them could yet name aloud to anyone else. Aldric had spent that time routing the Crown's Eyes' attention where his instincts, not his evidence, told him to look, had positioned certain alliances a little more carefully than their surface reasons required. Vesper, he knew only in shape and not in detail, had done a version of the same on his own side of the mountains — a readiness Aldric had never once asked to see the particulars of, because a king who demanded to inspect his most powerful ally's private preparations was not truly treating him as an ally at all. Better prepared and not need it than the reverse. It was as close to a shared creed as the two of them had ever spoken plainly to one another, on the one night decades ago when Vesper had told him, quietly and without any legal obligation to, that Morvath had survived the war after all. Aldric was flying north now on the strength of that same old trust, not blind to what he was asking, but not asking it of a man starting from nothing either.

Isolde was the part of the calculation he could not make sit easily, however many times he turned it. She had given him the figures that confirmed her own family was very possibly weeks or months from open war, had done it without flinching, without once asking him to soften what he did with the knowledge. He believed, as he had believed for over twenty years now, that her loyalty to this crown and to their children was real, was not some deeper performance laid over her father's ambitions. But belief was not proof, and there were people close enough to her — attendants, cousins, old family retainers she had never thought to question because she had known some of them since girlhood — who did not need her knowledge or her permission to carry word of what moved through this palace. He would not tell her he was flying north. Not because he doubted her. Because he could not risk what might be taken from her without her ever knowing it had been taken at all.

He went to see Solara himself rather than send for her.

She met him in the roost with the low, considering stillness she had always carried when she sensed something beyond routine in him, gold scales dulled slightly with disuse in a way that shamed him more than he expected it to. He had not flown her — truly flown her, distance and purpose rather than the short ceremonial circuits the court still occasionally required — in longer than he cared to count precisely. A king did not leave the capital. It was one of the first lessons of the crown, absorbed so early he had stopped noticing he carried it. His body remembered the motions of mounting her, the particular balance of weight and grip, in the dim, half-forgotten way a man remembers a language he learned as a child and rarely speaks. He pressed a hand to her neck and felt her breathe, slow and enormous beneath his palm, and thought, not for the first time, that this was very possibly the last uncomplicated thing left to him — a creature who required nothing of him but his presence, no calculation, no performance, only the plain fact of being there.

The rest of the arrangements took the better part of a day. He told the court he traveled north on a private matter concerning House Kaelarion's own holdings, true enough in its bones to survive scrutiny without inviting any. Dorian would hold the capital in his absence — an arrangement that cost Aldric a measure of private unease even now, after everything, though he told himself firmly that a few weeks of real responsibility, unsupervised, might do his elder son more good than another decade of careful correction ever had. Corvin found him in the roost as the preparations finished, quiet in the way Corvin always was, asking fewer questions than Dorian would have and accepting the answers Aldric gave him without pressing for the ones beneath them.

"Come back," Corvin said simply, before he left, and Aldric found it steadied him more than anything else said to him that day.

He launched from the capital in the grey hour before true morning, when the sky still held enough dark to blur detail but not enough to hide the shape of a golden dragon rising from the royal roost entirely. He felt the city's eyes on him as Solara climbed — not many, not yet, only the handful awake enough at that hour to notice and wonder, but enough. He had wanted that. Had built the entire wager on precisely that. Somewhere below, word would already be moving through channels he could not see and did not control, carried by people loyal to a house that was not his own, and there was nothing left to do about that now except let it move and trust that he had judged its shape correctly.

The mountains rose slowly ahead of him as the light came up properly, Blackspire's peak catching the first true sun long before the lowlands beneath it did, and Aldric found, climbing toward it, that whatever fear he had carried through the last sleepless week had settled, for now, into something steadier. He did not know exactly what waited for him at the end of this flight, or what it would cost either house before this was finished. He knew only that he had chosen to move first, on his own terms, rather than wait any longer to be moved against — and that whatever else came of it, Vesper Caldarion was already watching the horizon for him, warned as much as a careful letter folded inside his daughter's ordinary words could warn a man, and would hear the rest from him directly, the way it had always been between them, rather than secondhand or too late.
