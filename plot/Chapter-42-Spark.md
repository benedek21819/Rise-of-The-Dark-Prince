# Chapter 42: Spark

The way-station had burned from the inside out, which was the first thing Lucian noticed and the first thing he did not say aloud.

Valtor said it for him, crouched at what had been a storeroom door, one gauntleted hand resting against the blackened frame without quite touching it. "Fire took here before it took the roof. Someone opened this from inside before it ever reached the walls." He straightened, and behind the visor slit his voice carried the same flat patience Lucian remembered from Greyfen, though nothing else about this ground resembled Greyfen at all. "A raiding party does not usually know which door in a stranger's building is worth opening first."

"Someone told them."

"Someone told them." Valtor moved to the next ruin without hurrying, the way a man moves through a room he already half-understands — not searching, confirming. "Harrow's own people, most likely. Not all of them. One, perhaps two, paid or frightened into it, and either explanation is a problem for another day, because it is not the problem in front of us now."

Harrow's steward, a stooped grey man named Osric who had aged a decade in the eight days since the raid, stood at the edge of the ash with his hands knotted behind his back and a species of restraint on his face that Lucian recognized because he had worn it himself, once, watching Firsthold's market square. "My lord, give the word and every man Harrow has left will ride on Corvane's hall by morning. We have buried six of our own this month. I do not know how much longer I can hold them back from doing it themselves."

"You will hold them back," Lucian said, and did not raise his voice to say it, because volume was a tool for men who had run out of better ones. "Not because your grief is wrong, Steward Osric. Because a war fought on your own judgment, tonight, ends with Harrow burned to its foundations and Corvane still standing, funded by a hand neither of us has yet been permitted to name. I am not here to watch that happen. Give me until tomorrow."

Osric's jaw worked once, and then he bowed, low and unsteady, the bow of a man who had been waiting eight days for someone with the authority to tell him no and mean it kindly. "Tomorrow, then, my lord."

They rode for Corvane's hall at first light, under a plain banner rather than the black-and-silver of full war footing — Lucian's own choice, made the night before over Valtor's quiet, professional disagreement. "A banner of parley tells them we still believe this can end at a table," Lucian had said. "If I am wrong, I would rather be wrong in a way that costs us less than being wrong the other way would."

"And if they read the plain banner as weakness rather than restraint?"

"Then that is a mistake they make, Captain. Not one I make first."

Corvane's hall sat low and old against a grey hillside, a house that had clearly once had money enough to build something better and had not had it in a generation since. Lord Aldous Corvane received them in a hall too large for the handful of retainers scattered along its walls, a thin, grey-faced man in his middle years whose hands would not quite stay still on the arms of his chair, and Lucian understood within the first minute of speaking to him that he had ridden here expecting to negotiate with the wrong person.

"My lord Corvane." Lucian kept his voice level, the same register he had used across a table from a queen who gave nothing away. "House Caldarion does not come to punish. We come as the liege lord this Duchy answers to, to end a boundary dispute that has cost more blood in a month than in the whole of its history before it. Stand your men down. Return what can be returned. This ends here, tonight, without further cost to your house."

Corvane's mouth opened, closed, opened again — and for a moment something almost like relief crossed his face, the look of a drowning man shown a rope. Then his eyes flicked, involuntarily, toward a doorway at the hall's rear, and closed again into something guarded and afraid that had nothing to do with Lucian at all.

"It is not — " he began, and stopped himself, visibly, the way a man stops himself from finishing a sentence that would cost him his life. "It is not so simple a thing to end, my lord. There are... obligations. Older than this dispute. I do not have the freedom you believe I have."

Lucian had read enough reports across enough years now to recognize the particular shape of a man speaking with someone else's leash around his throat. He did not press it further in the hall — pressing it here, in front of Corvane's own retainers, would only cost the man whatever thin authority he had left to survive the conversation after Lucian rode away — but he understood, with the same cold clarity Dain had spent years building into him, that he had come to parley with a man who could not honestly promise anything, because the promise was no longer his to give.

"Then I have wasted a morning speaking to the wrong man," Lucian said quietly, and rose. "I hope, for your sake, that whoever holds the true leash here remembers you are still worth more to them standing than fallen."

They rode out within the hour, back along the logging road that cut down through timbered high ground toward the river crossing, and it was there, on the narrow wooden span over a gorge too steep to ford, that the ground itself turned against them.

The crossing went from beneath the lead riders' hooves in a single collapsing groan — not fire this time, but rope and pin, cut and pulled clean by hands that had clearly been waiting hours for exactly this moment — and the party split in the same instant the first arrows came out of the treeline on the near bank. Valtor and the bulk of the company found themselves fighting a real, if unremarkable, knot of Corvane's irregulars on solid ground, men with more courage than training who broke and reformed and broke again under the Guard's plain, contemptuous efficiency — not a fight the Silver Guard needed to fear, only one that needed time, and the collapsed crossing had bought their attackers exactly that.

Lucian went down with two men of his own escort onto a narrow shelf of rock below the ruined span, cut off from Valtor by twenty feet of shattered timber and a drop neither side could easily cross under fire, and understood within seconds that the men closing on him from the treeline above were a different kind of danger than the ones Valtor was cutting through on the ridge.

They did not come at him with drawn steel raised to kill. They came with rope, and a weighted net, and a low, disciplined voice somewhere behind them saying *alive, he is worth nothing to us broken* — and Lucian felt something in his chest go very still and very cold, the particular stillness of a man realizing that dying was not, after all, the worst thing being offered to him today.

His own two men fell fast, wounded rather than killed, dragged back and pinned before Lucian could reach either of them, and then there was only him, and six men moving with the flat, coordinated patience of people who had rehearsed this exact taking more than once before.

He never chose what happened next. It happened to him, the way falling happens to a man who has already lost his footing.

The world did not go dark. It went *sharp* — every sound around him suddenly separated out and legible, the particular creak of a boot shifting weight on wet stone, the specific inhale before a man commits to a lunge, the whisper-thin difference between a blade drawn to threaten and one drawn to strike — while at the very same instant the six men closing on him staggered, as one, into total blindness and total silence, hands going up to cover ears that could no longer hear anything at all, eyes open and finding nothing but black.

Lucian did not understand it. He did not have time to understand it. He only had time to use it, moving through six men who could neither see him coming nor hear the choices he made about which of them to put down and which of them to leave breathing, with a clarity that felt less like a gift than like something reaching into him and taking control of his own hands for a purpose that was not entirely his.

By the time the last of them went down — disarmed, not dead, because some smaller, colder part of him had known even in the middle of it that dead men could not be questioned — his own senses folded back into their ordinary shape all at once, sound rushing back in a flood that made him stagger, and he found he was breathing hard over six men on the rock shelf, three of them groaning and reaching for ears that worked again, and he had no memory of deciding to spare any of them. He had simply done it, the way his hand had simply moved.

That was when the shadow crossed the gorge.

Ancalion came down out of the high grey cloud where he had been keeping station since the parley, close enough that the wind of his wings reached the rock shelf before his claws did, and the men still capable of standing froze at the sight of him the way small animals freeze at a hawk's shadow — not fighting men confronting a fighting man's death, but men built for a quiet abduction suddenly facing something no coin they had been promised could have prepared them for. Two ran outright, scrambling up the loose scree without looking back. One did not run fast enough, and Ancalion's landing alone, thunderous and absolute, put him on his back in the mud before he had covered ten feet.

Across the ruined crossing, the same shadow and the same sound broke whatever nerve Corvane's irregulars had left. Valtor's men, already pressing a fight they were winning cleanly, needed only that final push; the retreat that followed was not a rout so much as a collapse, men throwing down half-drawn bows and scattering into the treeline in every direction that was not toward the dragon.

Valtor reached him minutes later than either of them wanted, forced the long way around the gorge's broken lip with four men at his back, and said nothing at all when he first saw Lucian standing alone over six restrained men and a landed dragon — only looked, for a long moment, with an expression Lucian had never once seen on him before, not fear exactly, and not awe, but the particular, careful recalculation of a soldier discovering that the ground beneath his feet was not quite the ground he had thought he was standing on.

"You are unhurt," Valtor said finally, and it was not entirely a question.

"I am unhurt." Lucian's voice came out steadier than he felt. His hands, he noticed distantly, had not stopped shaking since the sound rushed back in.

Valtor looked down at the three men on the rock shelf still restrained, then out toward the two who had made it into the trees, then back to Lucian, and something in his face settled, deliberately, into the plain language of duty rather than the harder questions underneath it. "Live prisoners. Taken to abduct you, not kill you." He said it flatly, the way he had once explained the doctrine of the cull, but there was a weight behind it now that had not been there at Greyfen. "That is worth more to your father than anything else this ground has given us today."

"I know." Lucian looked down at his own hands until they finally stilled. "Get them bound and moving, Captain. I will speak to my father about the rest of it myself."

Valtor held his gaze one moment longer than the order required — not disapproval, Lucian thought, but something closer to a man filing away a fact too large to address on an open hillside with wounded men still needing tending — and then he turned and gave the order, and did not ask the question either of them knew he wanted to ask.
