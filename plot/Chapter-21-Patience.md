# Chapter 21: Patience

The visit had five days left in it, and Lucian spent them the way his father had told him to.

Watch the steward. He speaks for the King's purposes before he speaks for the girl he escorts. Lucian had turned the instruction over enough times that it no longer felt like something given to him from outside — it had become a lens he carried without needing to remember he was holding it. He watched the steward the way he watched anything unfamiliar: without staring, without missing anything. The man was correct in every visible particular. He bowed at the right depth to the right people. He spoke of the crown's regard for House Caldarion in phrases smooth enough that they left no seam for a question to catch on. And twice, in five days, Lucian saw him step away from the ordinary business of the visit to speak alone with the Kaelarion escort commander, briefly, in the kind of low voice that was not raised in anger and was not meant to carry.

He did not learn what was said in either conversation. He learned that the conversations existed, and that no one troubled to explain them to a room that included a nine-year-old princess and the boy she was promised to.

Seren, in those same five days, decided that Lucian was hers to find.

It began reasonably enough. She sought him out after the second morning's training, waiting near the lower stair with her nurse a patient half-step behind, and asked him plainly whether Ancalion had flown that day, and whether he had felt it if he had. Lucian answered, because the question was easy and she had earned some measure of that ease in the roost two days before. It did not end there. By the fourth day she had found him in the library, in the colder gallery near the family wing, once even outside the training hall itself, waiting through the tail end of a session with an attention that did not waver no matter how plainly Dain's stare told her the hall was not hers to occupy.

“You are very serious,” she informed him, on the third occasion, apropos of nothing he had said.

“I am occupied.”

“You are always occupied.” She did not say this as a complaint, which somehow made it worse. “I only wanted to tell you that Korrath — House Dravenhall's dragon — can be heard breathing fire from nearly a mile off, even over the wind. I read that. Did you know that?”

“I did not need to know that.”

“Now you do.” She seemed satisfied with this, as though the exchange had gone exactly as intended, and left him to whatever he had been occupied with before she arrived.

By the fifth day it had stopped being merely tiring and had become, in the particular way small persistent things became larger than their size, genuinely difficult to bear. He was crossing the upper gallery on his way to the sparse hour Dain had granted him between the morning's forms and the afternoon's, the closest thing to solitude the visit had left him, and she was there before he had cleared the second archway, already speaking before he had fully registered her presence.

“I wanted to ask —”

“Not now.”

The words came out flatter and harder than he had intended, an edge he did not usually allow himself outside the training hall, and for a moment the gallery held an entirely different kind of silence than the one he was used to receiving from her. Seren's face did not crumple, exactly. It went carefully, deliberately still, the particular stillness of someone absorbing a thing they had not expected and refusing to let it show on the way in.

“You do not have to be cruel about it,” she said, evenly.

“I did not intend cruelty. I intended five minutes without being spoken to.”

“You could have said that instead.”

She was, he registered with some distant, unwelcome clarity, entirely correct. He did not apologize. He was not yet in the habit of it, and the moment had already closed itself around the omission before he found the shape of the words he might have used instead. Seren regarded him a moment longer, chin level, and then turned and went the way she had come without further comment, her nurse falling into step behind her with the particular silence of someone who had decided, wisely, not to remark on what she had just witnessed.

Lucian stood alone in the gallery for longer than the encounter had lasted. The quiet he had wanted was, now that he had it, less satisfying than he had expected.

It was two evenings later, on the last full day of the visit, that the fragments he had been collecting finally resolved into a shape.

He had gone looking for Lyra in the smaller solar near the family wing and found the door not quite closed, low voices reaching him before he had decided whether to knock. His father's voice, and beneath it, the steward's — a conversation that had clearly not been meant for the corridor, and certainly not for him.

“—the northern houses have not settled,” the steward was saying, careful even in privacy. “Dravenhall reads the crown's silence as invitation. His Majesty judged it wiser to be seen standing beside Caldarion now, plainly, before anyone mistakes the quiet for weakness.”

“The betrothal was already settled,” Vesper said. “It required no visit to reaffirm it.”

“The betrothal required nothing. The message required a visit. There is a difference, my lord, though I understand you would have preferred the King simply say so.”

A short silence followed, one Lucian could not read from the corridor.

“He might have,” Vesper said at last, dry in a way Lucian rarely heard from him. “It would have cost him less.”

Lucian did not linger at the door. He had heard enough to complete the shape his father's instruction had been pointing him toward for five days: the visit had never been only about Seren, or even mostly about her, however plainly she believed otherwise. It had been a piece of a larger, colder conversation between the crown and House Dravenhall, conducted in the shape of a child's visit to her betrothed because that shape asked no one to admit what it actually was. Seren herself, he understood with a clarity that sat oddly beside his earlier irritation, likely had no idea. She had asked to see a dragon. The rest had been built around her without her ever being told it was there.

He carried that unevenly matched knowledge with him to the lower courtyard the next morning, where the party assembled for departure in the same loose order it had arrived in five days before — escort first, steward correct and unreadable, the rest gathering their travel cloaks against a wind that had not softened at all in the interval.

Seren found him near the gate, her nurse a permitted distance back this time, as though even she understood this was the last exchange the visit had room for.

“I am still annoyed with you,” she said, without preamble, which he had by now come to expect was simply how she began most things that mattered to her.

“I am aware.”

“You should apologize properly. Not the way you almost did in the gallery, where you only explained yourself and called it enough.”

Lucian considered this. The wind pulled at both their cloaks, and behind her the steward was already directing the escort toward the outer gate with the same smooth, unreadable competence he had shown the whole visit, a man carrying a message neither of the children in front of him had been told they were also carrying.

“I was short with you,” Lucian said. “I should not have been, regardless of the reason.”

It was not elaborate. It was, he found, exactly as much as he was willing to give, and to his mild surprise it appeared to be sufficient. Something in Seren's face eased, not into the open, unguarded warmth of the roost two days before, but into something plainer and more ordinary — the look of a small accounting closed, rather than forgiven outright.

“I will still tell you things you do not need to know,” she said. “I only wanted you to say it properly first.”

“I expect you will.”

“You could pretend to mind less.”

“I could,” he agreed. “I am not certain I will.”

That, unexpectedly, drew something close to a laugh from her — short, surprised out of her rather than offered — before her nurse's hand found her shoulder and turned her toward the gathering party. She went without further argument, glancing back once before the gate took her, the same clear, pale blue eyes that had not looked away first in the reception hall five days before.

Lucian watched the party descend the Ascend until the last of the escort's dark cloaks had thinned into the general white of the mountain. Beside him, unnoticed until he spoke, Dain had come to stand at the edge of the courtyard.

“You heard something you were not meant to,” Dain said. It was not quite a question.

“Yes.”

“Your father knows you were near the door.”

Lucian looked at him. “He did not close it.”

“No,” Dain agreed. “He did not.”

Neither of them said anything further about what that meant. The wind moved across the empty courtyard, and Blackspire settled back over itself the way it always did once its visitors had gone — cold, ordered, unremarkable, as though five days of strangers and a King's quiet message had been nothing more than a draft through an open door.
