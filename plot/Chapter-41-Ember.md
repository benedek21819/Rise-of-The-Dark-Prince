# Chapter 41: Ember

The first report was easy to dismiss, and Lucian understood, reading it over his father's shoulder in the lower solar, exactly why it had taken three more before anyone at Blackspire had thought to connect them.

"Harrow and Corvane," Vesper said. "You will not know the names well. There has been no reason for you to. A boundary quarrel over a Cold Silver claim, older than either of our fathers, that has never once required anything of this house beyond a steward's letter telling both sides to behave themselves." He set the newest report atop the others, four now, each thicker and more urgent than the last. "Grazing land burned a month ago. A mine shaft's support timbers sabotaged three weeks past, nearly killing two of Harrow's own miners. A raid on a Harrow way-station eight days ago that left men dead on both sides, which an argument over old fence lines has never once produced in the whole of its history."

Lucian read them in the same unhurried, exact way Dain had taught him to read anything that mattered, and found, by the third page, that a shape was already forming beneath the words that had nothing to do with grazing rights at all.

"Corvane does not have the coin for this."

"No. Corvane has spent the years since its own humiliation exactly as poor and exactly as quiet as we intended it to remain, and poor, quiet houses do not suddenly arm raiding parties capable of taking a fortified way-station in a single night." Vesper's voice carried no surprise in it, only the flat, familiar weight of a pattern confirming itself precisely as he had feared it would. "I did not need Valtor's reports or Dorian's letters to tell me where this coin originates. I needed only to remember that a house we humbled once, rather than destroyed, is exactly the kind of instrument a patient enemy keeps in reserve for a day it finally has use for it."

"Dravenhall."

"Dravenhall does not care whether Harrow's mine changes hands. Dravenhall cares that House Caldarion is forced to spend attention, men, and coin putting out a fire lit inside its own Duchy, using a grudge that was always ours to manage and never theirs to touch." Something in Vesper's jaw tightened, controlled but unmistakable. "It is precisely the doctrine I once explained to you in this same room, turned back against us. Deniable. Untraceable to any hand that could be named aloud. And effective regardless, because burned grazing land and dead men at a way-station do not care whose coin paid for the burning."

Lucian set the reports down, and felt, beneath the cold clarity Dain had spent years building into him, something else entirely — a kind of anger that did not announce itself the way Riven's did, loud and immediate, but sat instead low and patient, the particular anger of watching an old wound be deliberately reopened by a hand too careful to ever be caught holding the knife.

"This cannot be managed the way the Firsthold thread was managed," he said. "Watched from a distance. Left to develop slowly, said nothing of."

"No. Men are dying on our own ground, in a dispute we are the liege lord over. Silence here is not caution. It is abdication, and every lesser house in this Duchy would read it correctly the first time we allowed it." Vesper's gaze held steady. "This must be resolved openly, as the ruling power of this Duchy resolving a vassal conflict — which is, I do not doubt, precisely the trap intended. Dravenhall cannot be named. Corvane can be. We are permitted to end this the way any liege lord ends an armed dispute between two houses beneath him, and no one beyond this room needs to know that the hand funding one side of it was never truly Corvane's own."

"You are sending me."

"I am." Something almost gentle crossed Vesper's face, brief and quickly folded away the way all his gentleness was. "You have led men in the field, and you have sat across a table from a king and a queen who gave nothing away, and until now those two things have never once been asked of you at the same moment. This will ask both. Corvane's men will not scatter simply because the heir arrives, the way a bear might. They know exactly whose blood they are shedding, and they have been given reason, however borrowed, to want to shed it. You will need Aurelia's judgment and Greyfen's steel in the same afternoon, more than once, before this is finished."

Lucian found Valtor within the hour, and this time the Captain's response to the assignment carried none of the measured neutrality of Greyfen's briefing — something sharper in the set of his shoulders, a soldier's plain recognition that the ground ahead was not wilderness but a house's own people turned against each other by a hand none of them could see.

"Fifteen," Valtor said, once he had read the reports through twice. "Fewer than Greyfen, and better chosen for it. This is not a cull. It is men facing men who know exactly who we are and exactly why they have been made to hate us, and a smaller company moving fast and visibly under your own banner will end this cleaner than a larger one grinding through it slowly ever could."

"You disagree with my father's choice to send me."

"I did not say that." Valtor's pale eyes held his, level and entirely without softness. "I said the ground is different, and the company must be shaped to match it. Whether you were the correct choice to lead it is not a question I have ever once heard your father ask himself twice, and I have long since stopped asking it on his behalf."

They rode out three days later, the North's short summer already beginning to thin toward the early cold that marked every season this far up the mountain's shoulder, Ancalion ranging ahead in long unhurried passes toward a stretch of contested border country Lucian had never once had reason to visit in fifteen years of calling this Duchy home. He found, watching the timber and stone give way by degrees to smaller, poorer holdings than anything Blackspire's own lands produced, that the unease sitting in his chest was a different creature than anything Greyfen had asked of him — not the old, animal dread of something large and hungry moving unseen through the trees, but the colder, more specific weight of riding toward people who had been given a real and particular reason to want him harmed, and who would not run from the trying.

Harrow's steward met them at the border of contested ground with a face already carrying the exhausted, grateful relief Greyfen's own people had worn on arrival, and behind him, half a mile further along the scorched tree line, the first columns of smoke from a burning way-station still rose thin and grey into the pale northern sky.

"They struck again three nights past, my lord," the steward said. "Worse than before. I do not know how a house as poor as Corvane has ever managed men and arms enough for this."

"I do," Lucian said, and did not elaborate further, though something in the flat certainty of it seemed to settle the steward more than any explanation could have. "Show me what is left."
