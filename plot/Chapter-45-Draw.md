# Chapter 45: Draw

The training yard's sand had not changed since the morning Riven and Thorne had first fought him together, but Lucian had, and so, in a way neither of his brothers yet fully understood, had the blade in his hand.

"That is new," Riven said, eyeing it with open, undisguised interest as Lucian settled into his opening stance. "Father's sword."

"Mine now." Lucian did not elaborate further, and something in the flat certainty of it seemed answer enough for both twins, who exchanged a glance that carried more understanding than either of them put into words.

Dain stood at the yard's edge where he always stood, arms folded, watching with the particular stillness of a man who had already decided what he wanted to learn from the next hour and was content to let the exercise teach it to him rather than announce it in advance. "Two falls," he said. "The same terms as before. I want to see how the sword changes what you already know, not whether you can still win without thinking about it."

The first exchange told Lucian more than a week of solitary drilling could have. The blade answered him — not dramatically, not in any way a bystander would have noticed, but in the small, private language of weight shifting fractionally toward wherever his focus settled, resistance easing by degrees when his intent was clean and certain, some faint reluctance creeping into a strike thrown from doubt rather than decision. Riven's opening rush met a parry that felt lighter in Lucian's hand than the same motion had ever felt with any blade before it, and he adjusted his own rhythm around that new lightness half a beat faster than his body was used to compensating.

"You're finding its edges," Thorne said, circling wide the way he always did, patient where his brother was not. "Or it's finding yours."

"Perhaps both."

The twins came at him with the same compounding pressure they had built across a year of fighting him together — Riven forcing the openings, Thorne closing them — and Lucian took the first fall in fewer exchanges than the last time, the sword making certain small mistakes cost him less than they once had, buying him the half-instants he needed to turn Riven's aggression back into Thorne's problem to solve. Thorne's own fall came harder-won, a longer exchange that ended only when Lucian read the exact moment his brother's guarded patience became guarded hesitation and closed the distance before hesitation could correct itself.

"Two falls," Lucian said, offering both of them a hand down. "The blade helped. I will not pretend otherwise."

"Good," Riven said, entirely unbothered, taking the hand. "Means we get to lose to something worth losing to."

It was Dain who spoke next, and something in his tone shifted the morning from ordinary sparring into the reason he had actually come to watch. "That is the sword. I did not bring you three out here for the sword." He looked at Lucian directly. "Your father told me what happened at the crossing. I want to see whether you can reach for it, deliberately, with two people willing to stand close enough to find out."

Riven's easy grin faltered slightly, replaced by something more careful. "Reach for what."

"Something Lucian will have to explain to you himself, if and when he chooses to," Dain said, before Lucian could answer for himself, and that was answer enough for both twins to understand the shape of it without being given the particulars — a family matter, not a training matter, the two kept carefully separate the way this house kept most things separate.

Lucian tried, first, exactly the way he had practiced alone in the days since Blackspire's quiet had settled back around him — standing still, reaching inward for the same sensation he remembered from the rock shelf, the sudden overwhelming clarity that had arrived without his asking for it. His own senses answered easily enough; that door had been open to him for years, the old terrace exercise Dain had built into him long before any of this, sound and sight sharpening at a thought the way a familiar muscle tightens on command. But the second door — the one that had, without his choosing it, torn sound and sight away from eleven armed men in a single instant — would not open for anything he tried in its place. He reached for it through stillness. He reached for it through the memory of the fear itself, deliberately recalling the particular cold certainty of being taken. Nothing answered. Riven and Thorne stood a few paces off, watching him with open curiosity and unimpaired senses, and after the fourth failed attempt Lucian found himself faintly, uncomfortably embarrassed in front of two brothers who had never once seen him fail at something in front of them before.

"It is not a lock," Dain said, watching him without any evident disappointment. "You cannot simply find the correct key and turn it. Nothing about this has ever been taught to anyone in this house, which means there is no wrong way to be doing it right now, only the ordinary difficulty of doing something no one can hand you the shape of in advance."

"Then let us make it harder to think about," Riven said, already loosening his shoulders again, some of the old restless energy creeping back into him. "Spar us again. Properly. Whatever it is, it did not happen to you standing still in a training yard. It happened while men were trying to kill you."

Dain considered this longer than Lucian expected him to, and then nodded once. "A measured bout. Not to injury. But do not go gently, either of you — if this is going to answer to anything, it will answer to something closer to what it answered to the first time."

The third exchange ran longer and harder than either fall before it, Riven pressing with real, unrestrained aggression and Thorne reading him closer to the edge of actual risk than either twin had allowed themselves in months, and it was there, in the compressed, urgent middle of an exchange Lucian was genuinely working to survive rather than solve, that something answered without his asking it to. It lasted less than a full breath. Thorne, mid-motion, blinked once, hard, his blade faltering for the length of a single heartbeat as though the world in front of him had gone briefly, inexplicably indistinct — and then it passed, and his strike came on exactly where it should have, and Lucian, startled by what he had just felt happen through his own hands rather than around him, took the opening only well enough to end the bout cleanly rather than decisively.

"Something happened," Thorne said afterward, sitting in the sand where the fall had put him, more curious than alarmed. "For a moment I could not quite see you. Not blind. Just — less certain the ground was where I remembered leaving it."

"That was me," Lucian said, and found, saying it aloud, that it did not feel like triumph so much as the plain, unsettling confirmation of something he had half hoped, even now, might have been a single unrepeatable accident.

Dain crouched beside Thorne, studying him with the same clinical care he studied any injury, and found nothing to study — no lasting harm, only a boy blinking the last of it away. "A fraction of a second. At close range. Under real pressure, not calm effort." He straightened, and looked at Lucian with an expression that held none of the easy praise he sometimes allowed after a clean bout. "That is not control. That is proof the door opens for more than one kind of danger. There is a very long distance between what just happened to Thorne and what happened to eleven armed men at that crossing, and I will not tell you otherwise to make this morning feel like more than it was."

"I know."

"Good. Because the honest answer is that I cannot teach you the rest of this the way I taught you Foundation, or Weight, or the sword in your hand right now. No one in this house's history has left behind a method for it. You will have to be patient with something that has no precedent, which is a different kind of patience than any I have asked of you before." Something almost gentle crossed his face, brief and quickly folded away the way it always was. "You found a fraction of a second today that did not exist in you a season ago. That is not nothing, Lucian. Most mornings, it will have to be enough."

Riven, still catching his breath a few feet away, looked between his brother and Lucian with an expression caught somewhere between unease and undisguised fascination. "I would like it understood," he said, "that if either of you expects me to be calm about my own brother momentarily losing his eyes in the middle of a spar, you have profoundly misjudged how calm I am capable of being."

"Duly understood," Lucian said, and found, for the first time since the crossing, that he could say it and almost mean the small, tired smile that came with it.
