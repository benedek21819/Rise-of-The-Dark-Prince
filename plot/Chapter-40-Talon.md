# Chapter 40: Talon

Vesper already knew.

Lucian understood it the moment he crossed the study's threshold, before his father had said a single word — the particular stillness of a man who had read a report twice and decided, in the reading, exactly how much of his reaction he intended to spend on it. Valtor's own account had outpaced the column home by the better part of a day, the same quiet efficiency that had once carried a rider's warning ahead of the twins' spar in Aurelia, and Lucian found, standing there, that he no longer expected anything he did in the field to reach Blackspire as a surprise.

"Sit," Vesper said. Not unkind. Not warm either.

Lucian sat.

"Valtor's account and Dressel's own both agree on the shape of it," Vesper said, "which tells me it happened exactly as plainly as it reads. You saw an opening the plan had not yet earned, and you took it, and a man who trusted your command paid for the gap it opened." He set the report down, and something in the careful, deliberate motion of the gesture carried more weight than raising his voice would have. "I am not going to repeat what Valtor has already told you. He was correct, and you have carried it home with you plainly enough that I do not need to ask whether the lesson landed."

"It landed."

"Good." Vesper studied him a moment longer, and something in his expression shifted — not softened exactly, but opened, in the narrow, deliberate way his father allowed himself to open at all. "I made a version of the same mistake once, young enough that I no longer remember whose life it cost, only that it cost one, and that the man who taught me the lesson afterward was considerably less patient with me than Valtor has been with you. I tell you that not to excuse what happened at Greyfen. Only so you understand that I am not judging you from a height I have never occupied myself."

Lucian had not expected that, and something in him settled slightly for the hearing of it, though he did not fully understand, sitting there, why his father's admission of an old failure should ease anything at all about his own recent one.

"You gave me the command anyway," he said. "Knowing what it might cost."

"I gave it to you because the only way to learn what your own instincts will do under real weight is to put real weight on them and watch. I would rather you learn this at fifteen, at Greyfen, with a broken shoulder as the price of it, than learn it later, somewhere the price is a life you cannot buy back with a season's healing." Vesper's gaze held steady. "You will lead again. Not because today was without cost, but because a commander who never fails at fifteen has usually only failed to be tested at all, and I did not raise you to be untested."

"Thank you," Lucian said, and meant it more plainly than the words alone carried.

"Do not thank me. Go and see Dressel is comfortable in the infirmary before you do anything else today, and then go be young for an hour, if you can still remember how. You have earned that much, whatever else today cost you."

Lucian found, leaving the study, that he did not entirely know how to obey the second instruction — until Selene solved the problem for him before he had crossed two corridors, materializing from the direction of the kitchens with the particular urgency of someone who had been lying in wait.

"You are back," she informed him, unnecessarily, already falling into step beside him. "And you did not forget."

"Forget what."

"The hawk." She said it with the patient, faintly offended clarity of someone repeating something that should never have needed repeating in the first place. "The one above the eastern wall. I told you about it before you left for Greyfen, and you said, and I quote you exactly, 'when I am back, we will go and see it properly,' and then you were gone nine days and came back with a war story about a bear instead of remembering the actual important thing."

"Nine days is not so long."

"Nine days is exactly as long as I counted them, one at a time, which makes it precisely as long as it needed to be to matter." She said it the way she said most facts that mattered to her, without apology for having kept the count at all. "Which means you owe me considerably more than a hawk at this point, but I am willing to accept the hawk as a start, provided you actually come this time instead of finding some excuse involving Dain or Father or a beast the size of a small mountain."

He looked down at her — smaller than she felt in the noise she made, one sleeve already smudged with something he did not have the energy to ask about, chin set in the particular determined angle she wore whenever she suspected an adult in her life was preparing to disappoint her — and found that whatever weight the study had left settled in him eased, not entirely, but enough to matter, simply from being looked at by someone who wanted nothing from him except an hour of his attention.

"Show me the way," he said.

She led him up through the eastern gallery and out along a narrow service stair he had not used since he was younger than she was now, chattering the whole climb about the hawk's habits, which she had apparently observed with a rigor that would have impressed even Dain — how it hunted mostly at dusk, how it had chased off a bird twice its own size the week before, how she had named it Talon despite Lyra's insistence that naming wild things only made it harder when they eventually left. The nest sat in a gap between two old stones near the top of the wall, visible from a narrow ledge that required, Lucian noted with some private alarm, considerably more careful footing than he suspected Selene had ever admitted to their mother.

"You have been coming up here alone."

"Only sometimes." She did not sound remotely sorry about it. "Lyra thinks I only come as far as the lower gallery. You are not going to tell her, because if you tell her she will make Cole watch me every time I want to come up, and then Talon will never come back with Cole standing there looking like a Silver Guardsman on duty."

"That is an excellent argument for exactly why I should tell her."

"It is a terrible argument and you know it." She grinned up at him, entirely unbothered by the contradiction, and pointed toward the gap in the stones where a dark shape had just settled, wings folding with a compact, economical grace that reminded Lucian, distantly and not unpleasantly, of Ancalion's own landings. "There. See? I told you she would come back before dusk if we waited long enough."

They stayed until the light went properly gold, Selene narrating every small movement of the hawk with the seriousness of a scholar presenting original findings, and Lucian found himself, somewhere in the unhurried length of it, laughing at something she said — quietly, almost by accident, the sound catching him faintly by surprise even as it left him.

"You laughed," Selene said, delighted, rounding on him at once. "I am telling Lyra. Not about the ledge. About the laugh. She will not believe me otherwise."

"You are not telling anyone anything."

"I am telling her exactly that, and you cannot stop me, because you are covered in bear and mountain and Father's stern voice, and I would like at least one person in this family to know that some of it washes off." She said it lightly, the way she said most things, and did not seem to notice how precisely she had landed on something true. "Come on. If we are quick I can show you where Riven hides the good bread before the kitchens notice it is missing."

Lucian let her pull him back down the narrow stair, the hawk settling into stillness above them and the last of the day's gold light thinning toward blue, and understood, following his sister's determined, chattering lead toward whatever small mischief waited next, that his father had been right about at least one thing today — he had, for the length of an hour on a cold stone ledge, remembered exactly how.
