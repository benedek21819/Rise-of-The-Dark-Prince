# Chapter 38: Call

The rider came in off the northern road two hours past midnight, half-frozen and swaying enough in the saddle that the gate guard sent for a healer before he sent word up to the keep at all, and by the time Lucian heard of it the next morning the whole of the account had already been written down, read twice, and carried to his father's desk.

"Greyfen," Vesper said, without preamble, when Lucian found him in the study. The word landed the way names of outlying settlements always landed in this house — familiar in the abstract, rarely spoken aloud unless something had gone wrong enough to require it. "A logging and trapping outpost, three days north along the high road, small enough that it answers to Firsthold's steward rather than keeping one of its own. They have lost eleven head of stock since the thaw. Two nights ago they lost a man."

Lucian took the report from his father's hand and read it in the same unhurried, exact way Dain had taught him to read anything that mattered. The account was plain, unembellished by whatever fear had shaped it in the telling — a trapper found half a mile from the outpost's fence, the ground around him torn in a way the writer had struggled to describe without simply calling it what it was. Tracks larger than any hand at Greyfen had seen in a working lifetime, though the prints themselves disagreed depending on who at the outpost had measured them, and no two witnesses could swear to the same number of beasts moving through the treeline, let alone what kind.

"Do we know what it is."

"Not with any certainty, and that troubles me more than a plain answer would." Vesper's voice carried no surprise in it, only the flat weight of a pattern he had clearly been watching longer than Lucian had known to watch it himself. "Wolf, bear, something else entirely grown past what anyone at Greyfen has ever had cause to measure against — the reports do not agree, and I have learned not to trust a frightened man's certainty about size or shape or number any more than I would trust his certainty about anything else in the dark. You have heard me speak of the wild beasts of the North before, in passing, the way one speaks of weather. This is what that weather sometimes produces, when the deeper mountains are left too long without anyone thinning what grows out there. I do not think I have ever told you plainly that the interval between hunts has been shrinking for two generations, and that no one in this house or in the Hall of Inquiry has ever proven why. Some say the deeper stock simply runs larger than the valleys ever did. Some say it is the Veil itself, some old scar in the world that predates our arrival here and has never fully healed. No one has ever settled the question. What is certain is simpler: something out there has grown too large and too bold to leave alone, and Greyfen cannot solve it with its own huntsmen."

"How long since the last hunt of any kind, in that stretch of the mountains."

"Longer than it should have been. Long enough, evidently, for this." Vesper set the report aside, and something in the deliberate stillness of the gesture told Lucian, before a word of it was spoken, that this was not a courtesy briefing. "You will lead the hunt yourself."

Lucian did not answer at once. He had learned, across the last several months of his father's quiet doors and quieter decisions, to let a thing like that settle fully before responding to it, and this one settled slower and heavier than most.

"Not merely attend."

"Lead. Command the party, set its pace and its plan once you have heard what the men who actually know that ground have to tell you, and answer for its outcome afterward — to me, and to Greyfen, and to whatever remains of the North's confidence in this house if it goes badly." Vesper's gaze held his, level and entirely without the softness he sometimes allowed in private. "What you have been doing these past months has been necessary and has been real, but it has also been invisible by design — decisions no one outside this study will ever know you made. This will not be invisible. Every household in Greyfen will watch you ride in, and every household in Firsthold will hear of it within the week regardless of how it ends. That is a different kind of weight than a sealed report burned the night it is read."

"Who rides with me."

"Valtor, and a company of the Silver Guard — twenty, perhaps a little more, depending on what he judges the ground requires once you have both studied the reports together. This is open strength, Lucian, not hidden work. It needs the right men, and someone willing to be patient enough to learn what is actually out there before Greyfen loses anyone else to guessing." Something almost like the ghost of amusement crossed Vesper's face. "Valtor will not command in your place. I have told him plainly that the party is yours to lead, and he does not need to be told twice what a command means once it has been given to him clearly. He will, however, tell you exactly what he thinks of every decision you make that he judges unwise, in precisely as many words as necessary and not one more. Use that. He has forgotten more about commanding men in the field than either of us has yet learned."

Lucian found Valtor within the hour, in the lower yard where the Silver Guard kept its own drills apart from Dain's hall, and the Captain received the news of the assignment with the same unreadable, weathered calm he brought to everything — a brief inclination of the head, nothing more, though something in the set of his shoulders afterward carried the particular attention of a man recalculating exactly how much care the coming weeks would require.

"I have read Greyfen's report," Valtor said. "I will have twenty ready to ride within two days, chosen for mountain work rather than formation drill. Whatever this turns out to be, it does not require an army to answer it. It requires men who can move fast and quiet through timber, and the patience to wait for a single right moment rather than force a dozen wrong ones."

"That is your judgment to make."

"It is. Yours is what we do once we are there, and how much of Greyfen's own knowledge of that ground we trust over our own instincts, however good those instincts may be." Valtor's pale eyes did not soften, but something in them carried a measure of approval Lucian had not expected to receive so plainly. "Lord Vesper was correct to give you this. I would not say so if I believed otherwise, whatever the house owed you by blood."

Riven and Thorne heard of the assignment before the day was finished — Lucian had not told them himself, which meant Vesper had, deliberately, and Lucian understood the deliberateness of it as its own kind of message the moment the twins found him in the corridor outside the armory, already half-argued into a shared certainty that neither of them intended to be left behind.

"We are coming," Riven said, which was not quite a request.

"This is not a training exercise."

"We are aware." Thorne's voice, quieter than his brother's, carried no less conviction for it. "We have trained for exactly this since before we could hold a blade steady. Father did not raise us to watch you ride into real danger from a window."

Lucian considered them both — Riven's restless readiness, Thorne's steadier, more measured certainty — and found, weighing the decision the way Valtor had just taught him to weigh one, that refusing them would cost more in trust than accepting them would cost in risk.

"You answer to Valtor's field orders the same as any Guardsman," he said. "Not to me alone, and not to each other, if the two of you disagree about what a moment requires."

"Understood," they said together, and something in the simultaneity of it, unplanned and entirely unconscious, reminded Lucian of the two falls it had taken him a full morning to win from them in Aurelia.

They rode out on the third day, Valtor's chosen twenty in the same full Cold Silver plate the Guard wore standing post within Blackspire's own walls — muted, undecorated, built for battle rather than display, the mountain's own cold no more a concern to it than an enemy's blade would be. The twins mounted near the column's center, Ancalion aloft and ranging ahead in long, unhurried passes that covered in an hour what the column would need most of a day to cross on the ground. The high road north of Firsthold narrowed by degrees into packed snow and then into little more than a hunter's track between close-standing pine, the mountains crowding closer on either side until the sky itself became a pale, narrow ribbon overhead.

They reached Greyfen on the evening of the third day, and the outpost that met them was smaller and more frightened than any report had fully conveyed — a scattering of timber halls behind a fence built for weather rather than war, faces at every window and doorway turning to watch the column arrive with an expression Lucian recognized, slowly, as something closer to desperate hope than mere curiosity. The steward who came out to meet them was an older man, weathered past his years, and he did not waste time on the formalities Lucian had grown used to receiving in Aurelia's marble halls.

"You will want to see the trapper's body before anything else," he said. "What is left of it. We kept it from the ground as best we could, for exactly this reason, though I will tell you plainly, my lord, that what is left will not make for an easy first look at what we are dealing with."

Lucian looked past him toward the treeline beyond the fence, dark and close and entirely indifferent to the fear it had put into every face behind him, and understood that whatever the reports had prepared him for, they had not prepared him for enough.

"Show me," he said.
