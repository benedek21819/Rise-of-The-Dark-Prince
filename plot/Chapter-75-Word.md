# Chapter 75: Word

He slept four hours on the roost floor and woke to Ancalion breathing in the same slow way he had been breathing since they got him up the cliff, which was as much as anyone was going to promise today.

The keepers opened the flank at first light and packed it again. The head keeper looked at it for a long moment and said nothing whatever about it, which Lucian decided to take as neutral.

Then he went down to the keep, because he had not once in a day and a half gone to anybody, and everybody had come to him.

---

His mother was in the small solar with the shutters open and the cold coming in, and she got to him before he was properly through the door.

He had seen her twice already — in the physician's room while the stitches went in, when he had been in no condition to say anything sensible, and again at some point in the night when he had half woken in the roost to find a blanket over him and had gone straight back to sleep without working out who had put it there. He had worked it out since.

She did not say anything for a while. She held onto him hard enough to hurt the stitched side of his face and he did not mention it, and after a moment he put his arms round her, which he had not done since he was about fourteen.

"You are filthy," she said eventually, into his shoulder.

"Yes."

"And you have not eaten."

"No."

"Then you will do both those things before you do anything else." Elara let go of him and held him at arm's length and looked at the stitched side properly, in daylight, which she had not been able to do the night before.

"It is clean," she said.

"He says it will hold."

"It will scar."

"Yes."

She did not say anything to that. She put her hand flat along the unmarked side of his face for a moment, and then let it drop, and told him to sit down.

Selene was on the window seat with her knees up, and had not moved, and was watching him with the particular flat attention of a child who has decided that if she asks the wrong question she will be sent out of the room.

"You can ask," he said.

She took him at his word.

She had watched the whole of it from the eastern gallery, where the household is not supposed to go and where every single person in the household had apparently been standing on the second morning. She had seen Lirath go down. She had seen Ancalion come up out of the valley — she had known it was him before anyone told her, because nothing else in the sky is that color — and she had watched the two of them go at Korrath over the roosts for the better part of an hour.

And she had seen the other one.

"It came out of the cliff," Selene said. "Under the roosts. The dark part where nobody goes."

Elara had gone very still by the fire.

"Yes," Lucian said.

"What is it?"

"Ours."

Selene considered that. She was thirteen and she was not a fool, and she recognized an answer that had been shaped to be an answer without being one, and she looked at him for a long moment deciding whether to press.

"Is it going to stay down there?"

"I think so."

"Does Father know about it?"

"Yes."

"Did *you* know about it?"

"No," Lucian said. "Not until it came out."

That, oddly, appeared to settle her more than anything else he could have said. She sat back against the window and let out a breath and said, "Good," and did not explain what was good about it, and asked instead whether Ancalion was going to be able to fly, which he could answer honestly and did.

He ate, because his mother stood over him until he had. Orlena came in halfway through, looked at him, put a hand briefly on the back of his neck as she passed, and sat down by the fire without a word about any of it.

---

He went down to his father in the middle of the morning.

It took four hours: down the second gully, along the mine track, then up the scree on foot at the end, which was the part that made his legs shake. He had stood at a rail the night before and looked at that light and known there was no way to reach it. In daylight it was simply a long walk.

Lirath was enormous on the loose stone, silver going grey with dust, propped where they had built the ground up under her shoulder with sacking and stone to take the weight off the ribs. There was a canvas half-rigged over her head against the weather. Two keepers were asleep under it.

Vesper was sitting on a rock at her shoulder with his coat over his knees like a man at the end of a long walk of his own.

Lucian sat down beside him and for a while neither of them said anything at all.

"The hip," Vesper said eventually.

"Riven said the physician did not like it."

"He does not. He will not say why, and I have not pushed him, because he will say why in a week and until then it would only be a guess with my name on it." Vesper looked along the length of her. "Three ribs. Four, possibly. The shoulder is filthy work but it will close. She has been drinking, which he says matters more than any of the rest of it."

"The wing?"

"Still under her." A pause. "She will not shift. He does not want to make her, and I do not want to make her, and so we are both waiting on an animal to decide something."

"And yours," Vesper said.

"Holding. They closed the flank last night and opened it again this morning and did not tell me anything either way." Lucian looked at his hands. "They have not touched the wing."

"They will not for some days yet."

They talked about it for a good while longer, the way men talk about things they can measure — the packings, the linen, the wing membrane and how it knits and what it does when it knits badly, the foreleg, whether the shoulder would take weight by the turn of the season. Neither of them mentioned a stone bridge or a field or the two hundred feet of air between them the morning before.

Lucian was aware, the whole time, that they were not mentioning it. He assumed his father was too.

---

Eventually he said what he had walked four hours to say.

He gave it in order. The second roost opened for the wounded. The prisoners kept as Caldarion's own and fed out of Caldarion's stores. The second gate opened without permission to the people burned out of the lower quarters. The four thousand in the valley to be burned west of the river, by their own countrymen, because there was no one else and it needed doing before the ground froze.

He heard himself explaining the reasoning behind the third one, and stopped, because he had not intended to explain anything.

Vesper listened to all of it without interrupting.

Then he said: "Yes."

That was all.

Lucian waited for the rest of it, and there was no rest of it, and after a moment his father shifted his weight on the rock and looked out over the valley.

"You will stop doing this," Vesper said.

"Doing what."

"Coming to me with it." He said it without any weight at all, the way he said most things that mattered. "You did not need me yesterday. You are not going to need me for that kind of thing again, and it does neither of us any good for you to walk four hours down a mountain to have me tell you that you were right."

Lucian did not say anything.

"Bring me the ones that would change the house," Vesper said. "Bring me anything with a crown in it or a border in it or a hundred years in it. The rest is yours. It has been yours since yesterday morning, whether either of us had noticed or not, and I would rather say so now than let you go on asking."

He got up off the rock, slowly, because he was a man who had held a field to its limit two days ago and had not slept properly since, and he put a hand flat for a moment on Lirath's neck.

"We have to write to the King," he said. "Come up when you have rested. Do not do it on those legs."

---

The letter took most of the evening, and Lucian sat through the whole of it.

His father wrote it himself, which he did not always do, and he wrote it four times. The first two went in the fire.

It said that Lord Roderic Dravenhall had been taken alive on Caldarion ground and was held at Blackspire, unable to travel, and would be delivered south as soon as he could be moved. It said the dragon Korrath was dead. It said the Dravenhall host had broken on the second morning and was moving south in pieces without its lord, and gave the number as four thousand dead in the valley and four hundred prisoners taken, and said what was being done about both.

It gave Firsthold's count in full. Vesper did not soften it and did not round it.

Near the end he wrote three sentences that took him longer than the rest of the letter together.

*A third dragon of this house was seen on the second morning by the garrison of the summit, by a considerable part of Firsthold, and by the enemy. He was recognized by Lord Roderic, who fought that war. I judged Your Grace would wish to know how far this has already gone rather than how it came to happen, which Your Grace has known these twenty-six years.*

He read it back, and did not change it, and passed it across without comment.

Lucian read it twice.

"You are not explaining it."

"No."

"He knows."

"He has known since I was twenty. He does not need the story; he needs the shape of the damage, and he needs it from me before he gets it from somebody in his own hall in a fortnight." Vesper took the page back and reached for the sand. "Never explain a thing to a man who already knows it. It makes him wonder why you thought he did not."

---

His own letters he wrote afterward, alone, in his own room, with his door shut.

Lyra's was easy. She was six days away at Tidewatch with a husband and a household and no part in any of this, and she would have heard something by now, and whatever she had heard would be wrong in the direction of worse. So he told her the truth in plain order, because she had never in her life thanked anyone for softening anything: the siege, the two days, Lirath down and mending, Ancalion torn and mending, the stitches in his face and how ordinary they were. He told her about the apple woman at the second gate, because Lyra would remember her.

At the end he wrote that their father was tired in a way he had not seen before and that she was not to come north until Thaw, because the roads were closing and there was nothing here she could fix by being in it.

Then he took a fresh sheet for Seren, and sat looking at it for a long time.

---

In the autumn he had sent her three pages about the light in the North and the first snow being late, and had put nothing of the war in it, and had told himself that this was mercy. She would know soon enough, and could do nothing with it from Aurelia, and there was no sense handing a girl a thing she could only carry.

He had also written, at the bottom of it, *I intend to be better. I would rather tell you that now than tell you later.*

She would have that letter in front of her within the month, next to whatever her father's court told her had happened in the North. And she would put the two side by side, because she was fifteen and clever and had been putting things side by side since she was nine.

So he wrote her the truth.

He wrote that Dravenhall had come to Firsthold with a siege train and forty thousand men, and that he had flown against them on the first day and on the second, and what that had cost the city. He wrote that Korrath had come over the mountain and that Lirath had been brought down and was alive. He wrote that Ancalion had been badly hurt and would mend, and that his own face had been opened on a rock and would leave a scar, and that she should be told that by him and not by a courtier making it worse.

He wrote that Roderic Dravenhall was alive and in a cell under this house and would be sent south to her father in the spring.

He did not write about her aunt. That was not his to hand her.

And he did not write about the last quarter of an hour on the shelf.

He sat for a while over it with the ink drying on the nib. Then he wrote, *There is one more thing I will not put in a letter. I would rather say it to you, and I will, when the roads open and I come south* — and he read that back and understood that he had just promised to tell her, in the spring, out loud, and that he had done it deliberately, and that some part of him had wanted to make it impossible to avoid.

He signed it and sealed it and put it with the others.

---

The courier went down the Ascend a little after first light, with three letters in his bag and the roads still open, and Lucian watched him out of sight from the second gate.

Three days to Aurelia if the weather held. Three back.

There was nothing whatever to do about any of it for six days, and there was a very great deal to do about everything else, and he went back up to see what the morning had brought.
