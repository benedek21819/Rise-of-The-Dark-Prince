# Chapter 68: Ash

The host took two days to come up the valley and another to arrange itself, and Lucian spent all three of them on the mountain with nothing whatsoever to do.

That was the part nobody had thought to warn him about. He had expected fear and found instead an unbearable, humiliating idleness — the household running around him in its quiet ordered way, Valtor's men going up and down the Ascend, his father in the solar with maps and couriers, and Lucian standing at windows. Ancalion felt it through the bond and gave it back doubled, restless in the roost, turning in the dark and settling and turning again.

From the upper wall he watched Roderic invest the city. It could not be surrounded, and Roderic did not try; the mountain held the north of it and always had, so the host spread itself in a long shallow arc across the open ground before the southern wall, and its shape from above was the shape of a hand laid flat against a door. The siege train came up behind it over two days, oxen and timber and the long covered shapes of engines, and by the third evening the fires of the camp ran further along the valley floor than Firsthold's own lights did.

His grandmother found him on the terrace on the second night and stood with him a while without saying anything, which he was grateful for. Selene asked him twice whether he was going down, and he told her the truth both times, which was that he did not know.

---

They came at the wall in the grey of the fourth morning.

He heard it before he saw it — a sound that reached the summit thin and flattened by distance, less like fighting than like weather, and it took him a moment to understand that what he was listening to was several thousand men shouting at once. Then the engines started, and that carried better.

He watched the first assault from the wall-walk with his hands on the stone.

At that distance it was not people. It was movement: a dark grain flowing forward across the open ground toward the southern wall, thickening at two points, breaking at one and reforming, and the small bright flowers of fire where the engines' shot came down inside the city. He could see the gate. He could see the roofs beyond it, the market row, the tight dark streets he had walked at fourteen with Lyra beside him, close enough to make out where the smoke was coming from and too far to make out anything else.

The second assault came an hour later, and it went further.

He knew what was in those streets. Dain had put it plainly in the solar with his cloak half on: the ones who would not go, who would bar their doors and bank their fires and wait for the house to be right, because it had never in living memory occurred to any of them that it might not be. Sixty thousand people who had been told for two hundred years that House Caldarion kept the North safe, and had believed it the way they believed the sun came up.

They were still down there. They had stayed because of his family's name, and they were burning inside the walls his family had built for them, and he was standing on a mountain, safe, at a distance, watching it happen with his hands on cold stone.

He did not decide anything. That was the part he would think about afterward — that there was no moment he could point to, no weighing, nothing he could later have argued with. He simply found that he was already moving, and that the thing in his chest had stopped being idleness some while ago and become something with a much shorter name.

Ancalion was standing when he reached the roost, awake, turned toward the entrance, wings already half-open — had been for some time, from the look of him. Lucian did not have to say anything at all.

---

They came down the mountain fast enough that the wind took the sound out of his ears.

The valley opened underneath him and the shape he had watched all morning stopped being a grain and became men — running, standing, hauling, thousands of upturned faces catching the grey light as the shadow went over them — and Ancalion took the first pass low along the length of the massed rear where the reserves stood packed and waiting to feed the assault.

Black fire has no glow to it. That was the thing Lucian had never properly understood until he saw it fall on a crowd: it did not light what it touched, it took the light out, a long rolling absence pouring down the line with the sound of a held breath let go, and where it went the noise of the army simply stopped and then started again much louder and in a different register altogether.

They came around. They went down the line again.

The second pass went through the engines. Kestrion timber, seasoned and oiled and stacked in the open with the crews around it, and it went up so fast and so completely that Ancalion had to bank hard through his own smoke, and Lucian felt the heat come up through the harness against his legs.

By the third pass the field had changed. Whatever the army had been doing before, it was not doing it now: the assault on the wall had come apart, men streaming back from the ditch, officers on horses trying to hold something together in the middle of it. Ancalion turned over the wreck of the reserve with his wings spread wide and the whole of the valley floor moving underneath in a way Lucian had never seen anything move, and Lucian understood, with a clarity that arrived before any feeling attached to it, that he could do this all day and there was nothing down there that could stop him.

Then the arrows came.

Not many at first — someone shouting, someone else pointing, a scattering that went nowhere. Then more. Then a great deal more, and all at once, as six thousand missile troops finished discovering what they were for, and the sky in front of them turned briefly solid.

Ancalion rolled out of it hard enough that Lucian's vision went dark at the edges. Two shafts came off the black scale of his shoulder without marking it and one did not — high on the wing where the membrane thinned, a punched hole the size of a fist that Lucian felt through the bond as a bright clean line of wrong.

They went up. High, and out, and around the shoulder of the mountain where nothing could follow.

---

He took them back twice more before the light went.

Both times the field had changed again. The army had learned by the second, and by the third it had stopped being an army in any shape worth burning — spread thin, dispersed across half the valley in loose ragged clumps with the archers standing among them, offering nothing anywhere that was worth the cost of a low pass. The fourth pass killed perhaps a tenth of what the first had, and cost twice as much doing it, and when Ancalion came around the last time Lucian felt the tiredness in him like a hand pressing on his own chest and turned them for the mountain without being asked.

Somewhere in the middle of the third pass he had reached for the Absence.

He was not certain afterward why he had — something about the archers, some half-formed idea about being harder to fix on — and it had opened for him and held the whole way down the line while the sky came apart around them, longer and wider than anything he had managed standing still on solid ground in a quiet room. He noticed it distantly at the time and lost it again in everything else.

It was only much later, lying awake, that he came back to it and found the shape of what was actually strange. Not that it had worked. That it had cost him nothing. He had held a door open through four minutes of that, and afterward there had been no drag, no ache behind the eyes, none of the flat grey exhaustion that a fraction of it had always bought him before.

He turned it over for a while in the dark, and could not make anything of it, and eventually stopped trying.

---

Ancalion slept badly with the wing dressed, and Lucian sat with him in the roost until well after midnight rather than go up to a bed he was not going to use.

From the landing platform he could see the valley. The camp fires were still there — fewer, scattered wider, but there — and Firsthold's own lights burned in the places where the fires had not gone out on their own yet, and between the two there was a long dark stretch of ground he had spent the afternoon making.

He had killed, by any count he could put on it, some thousands of men in the space of an afternoon.

He waited to feel it. He sat in the cold with his back against the warm scale of his dragon's flank and waited, honestly and without cheating, for whatever was supposed to arrive to arrive.

What came instead was the arithmetic. That the second pass had been worth four of the fourth. That he had gone in too low the first time and had simply been lucky. That if he had held the third pass thirty seconds longer he would have caught the officers with the reserve instead of after it, and that thirty seconds would have been worth several hundred men, and that he had not held it because he had not thought of it in time.

He sat with that for a long while, and with the fact that it was the only thing that had come, and eventually he stopped waiting.

Above him, higher up the black shoulder of the mountain, the keep showed one lit window where his father was still awake.

Tomorrow they would come at the wall again.
