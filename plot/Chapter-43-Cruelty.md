# Chapter 43: Cruelty

Vesper listened to the whole of it without once interrupting, which was somehow worse than if he had.

They sat in the lower solar, the same room where the first reports on Harrow and Corvane had been laid across the desk a lifetime ago, and Lucian gave the account plainly, the way Dorian's own instructors might have judged a report — the failed parley, Corvane's frightened, hollowed-out lord, the collapsed crossing, the eleven men who had come for him with rope rather than steel. He made himself say the last part carefully, because he did not yet have better words for it than the plain ones. "Something happened to me on that rock shelf. I did not choose it. It happened, the way falling happens."

"Tell me exactly what you remember."

Lucian told him — the sudden, terrible sharpness of his own senses, and the same instant taking everything from the men closing on him, sight and sound both, until they were fighting a battle they could no longer perceive at all. Vesper's face, through all of it, did not move the way Lucian had half-expected it to. There was no pride in it, no relief at a question finally answered. Only a stillness that Lucian recognized, because he had worn a version of it himself over the last three days without knowing what to call it.

"You understand what you are describing," Vesper said finally.

"My Domain."

"Your Domain." Vesper rose, crossed to the window, and stood with his back to the grey light for a moment before he spoke again, and when he did, his voice had gone quieter than Lucian had heard it in some time. "I told you once that it emerges from what its bearer already is, magnified. I did not tell you what that meant, because I did not know yours, and because it is not a kind thing to tell a boy in advance — that whatever answer the Veil gives him about his own nature, he will have to live inside it afterward, whether he likes the shape of it or not." He turned back. "Mine came to me as a young man standing over a battlefield, wanting nothing in this world so much as to make everyone in it slow down long enough for me to think. Yours came to you bound on a rock shelf, being taken by men who meant to make you disappear. I do not think that is a coincidence, Lucian. I think the Veil showed you exactly what it does when something tries to take you against your will — it makes you the only person left who can still see."

Lucian said nothing to that for a long moment, because there was nothing yet he trusted himself to say.

"How do you feel, since?"

"Steady." It was not entirely true, and he suspected his father knew it was not entirely true, but it was close enough to stand on. "My hands shook for an hour afterward. They have not shaken since."

"That will do, for now." Vesper's gaze held his, level and unhurried. "We will speak of controlling it later, once there is something to control besides the memory of it. Today there is other work."

The nine men were held in the old stone cells beneath the eastern wall, and Lucian did not go down to question them himself — his father's people handled that quietly, thoroughly, and without a name Lucian was given or asked for, the same faceless efficiency that had always handled what could not be done in daylight. What came back to the solar by evening was not a confession so much as a confirmation of everything the collapsed crossing had already told them without words: Lord Aldous Corvane had given no such order and possessed no such authority. The men had been recruited, armed, and paid through three careful layers of go-betweens that traced, in the end, to the same deniable network first uncovered watching the coin behind Firsthold — the same hand, closer to the bone this time. Burning a way-station was theft of attention and resources. Taking the heir of Caldarion alive was something else entirely, and both of them understood, reading the report together in silence, precisely how much further that hand had just reached.

"They will not be questioned twice," Vesper said, when the reading was done. "There is nothing left in them worth a second asking. What remains is what to do with them now, and I am not going to decide that for you."

Lucian looked up. "You want me to choose."

"I want you to choose, and I am not going to tell you what I would choose, because it would stop being your judgment the moment I did." Vesper's expression gave away nothing of what answer he was hoping to hear, though Lucian did not fully believe, even then, that there was no answer his father was hoping for. "You have led men in the field. You have sat across from lords who lied to your face and princes who resented you for existing. This is smaller than either of those things and heavier than both of them combined, because there is no politics left to hide behind. Nine men tried to take you from this house. What happens to them is yours to decide."

Lucian sat with it longer than he had sat with anything in recent memory — not because the shape of the decision was unclear to him, but because he wanted, deliberately, to be certain of the reason he was reaching for it before he let himself reach.

He thought of Greyfen, and the doctrine of the cull, patient and impersonal, killing only what needed killing and nothing more. He thought of the raiding parties burning Harrow's grazing land, men fighting a war that was never truly theirs, driven by borrowed coin and an old grudge that predated most of them — men he might, in a different chapter of this, have found room to spare. And he thought of eleven men closing on him with rope and a net and a voice behind them saying *alive, he is worth nothing to us broken*, men who had known exactly whose life they were reaching for and had chosen the taking of it as a trade worth making.

"Execution," Lucian said. "All nine."

Vesper did not flinch, and did not soften, and asked only the one question that mattered. "Why."

"Because they were not fighting a border dispute. They came to take me — to make me a piece on someone else's board, alive and useless to everything I am meant to become. If I let that stand as a risk with a survivable cost, I teach every enemy this house will ever have that taking me is a door worth trying again." His voice did not rise, the way it had not risen with Osric at the ash of the way-station, the way it had not risen across the table from a frightened, ruined lord who could no longer speak for his own men. "This is not punishment for what they did to me personally. It is the price of what they attempted against this house, made plain enough that no one repeats the attempt believing it might end differently."

For a moment Vesper said nothing at all, and something in his face — not pride exactly, Lucian thought, but something adjacent to it, older and more complicated — settled into stillness. "Then it is done. I will see it carried out by morning."

Neither of them spoke for a while after that, the solar gone quiet around the two of them and the fire burned low, and Lucian found, sitting there, that the decision had cost him something he had not fully expected it to cost — not doubt, not regret, but a kind of weight settling permanently into a part of himself that had not carried it an hour before. He understood, without being told, that this was not a weight he would ever fully set back down again.

It was Vesper who broke the silence, and when he did, his voice had changed register entirely, moved from the careful measure of a father testing a son's judgment into something heavier and more deliberate — a ruler laying the next stone before the last one had even finished settling.

"There is a second matter, and I will not soften the size of it for you." Vesper's eyes did not leave his son's face. "House Corvane still stands. I made that choice once before, in your boyhood — to humble rather than destroy, to leave a broken house breathing rather than erase it from this Duchy entirely. You have seen, these last days, exactly what that mercy cost us. A house left standing with a grudge and no resources of its own is a door any patient enemy can eventually walk through, and Dravenhall has now walked through it twice."

"You want to destroy them."

"I want you to decide what becomes of them, the same as I wanted you to decide what became of the nine men in the cells below us." Something in Vesper's voice made it clear this was no smaller a burden than the first, and very possibly larger. "Lord Aldous Corvane, his house, his lands, and every soul still living under his roof who has done nothing but be born beneath a banner Dravenhall found useful to corrupt. I will not tell you what I would choose. I chose already, once, and you have spent the last three days learning exactly what that choice bought us." He rose, and for a moment the weight of the entire Duchy seemed to settle across his shoulders in a way Lucian had rarely seen him allow to show. "Sleep on it if you must. But the decision is yours to carry, Lucian, and it will not wait forever."
