# Chapter 23: Roots

The road south took four days, and by the second Lucian understood that his mother had not been exaggerating when she once said the cold followed him rather than the other way around.

It began to loosen somewhere past the lower passes — the wind first, losing its edge by degrees until it no longer cut through the seams of his cloak, then the light, softening from the hard steel-grey of the North into something with actual warmth in it. By the third day there was green. Not the stubborn, frost-edged green of Blackspire's walled gardens, but broad, careless green — hedgerows, standing crops, trees in full leaf that had never once had to fight the mountain for the right to exist. Selene had her face pressed to the carriage window for most of that day, narrating the landscape to no one in particular.

“There are so many colors,” she said, for perhaps the fourth time. “Lucian, look. It's not even trying to be pretty. It's just like that.”

“I see it,” Lucian said.

“You don't sound like you see it.”

He did not have a ready answer for that, and did not attempt one. Elara, across from them, watched the window with a stillness that was not quite the composed stillness of Blackspire — something looser had entered her posture the further they traveled, as though degrees of latitude were also degrees of permission.

They reached the Thalorin seat on the fourth afternoon, a wide manor of pale stone set above a bend in a slow green river, its grounds unwalled in a way that would have been unthinkable at home. Lucian noted the absence of high defensive walls the way he noted anything unfamiliar, and understood, before anyone told him, that this was itself a kind of statement — a house that did not need to look dangerous to survive.

Lord Halric received them in the outer courtyard, dressed in soft blue and silver rather than black, his bow to Elara deep and genuine before he turned the same courtesy, more measured, toward her children.

“Lucian,” he said, studying him with an attention that was not unlike Vesper's, though it wore a different face — less weighing a blade, more assessing an investment already well underway. “You have grown since the betrothal portraits.”

“I am told that happens, my lord.”

Something in Halric's mouth moved, not quite a smile, closer to approval of the dryness itself. “So it does.”

Lady Ysara did not wait for the courtesies to finish. She came around her husband with both hands already lifting, and it was Selene she reached for first, sweeping her into an embrace that lifted the girl half off her feet and drew a delighted, undignified shriek that would have earned a corrected posture and a quiet word from Lyra anywhere within Blackspire's walls. Here, no one corrected it.

“Look at you,” Ysara said, setting her down only to hold her at arm's length and look again. “Filthy already, and we've only just arrived.”

“I fell by the river gate,” Selene said, with no apparent shame at all.

“Of course you did.” Ysara laughed, bright and unguarded, and only then turned to Lucian. Her expression did not change so much as settle into something quieter, more careful, as she looked at him the way a healer looks at an old injury that has been left to close on its own.

“Lucian,” she said. She did not embrace him the way she had Selene. She seemed to understand, correctly, that he would not know what to do with it. She took his hand instead, both of hers around it, warm and dry and steady. “You have your mother's eyes when you're thinking. Did you know that?”

“No, my lady.”

“Grandmother,” she corrected, with a firmness that had no argument built into it at all. “Not my lady. Not here.”

He did not quite know what to do with that either, and said nothing, and she seemed to find that answer sufficient in its own way, patting his hand once before releasing it.

Elias arrived last, unhurried, dressed for a court rather than a courtyard, and inclined his head to Lucian with the easy, assessing warmth of a man who had already decided the conversation would be useful regardless of how it went.

“The famous nephew,” he said. “I confess I expected someone taller.”

“I am told that happens as well,” Lucian said. “Eventually.”

Elias's laugh was quick and genuine. “Good. He has Vesper's edge and enough sense to aim it somewhere harmless. Come — walk with me before supper. I want to hear what the North makes of the crown's sudden interest in weddings it already agreed to years ago.”

Lucian did not mistake the lightness of the question for the absence of a real one beneath it. He answered it carefully, giving Elias roughly the shape of what he had pieced together in the gallery outside his father's solar — Dravenhall's restlessness, the crown's need to be seen standing beside Caldarion rather than simply being told it stood there — and Elias listened with the particular attention of a man filing every word away for later use, nodding at points Lucian had not expected to matter and passing lightly over ones he had thought would.

“Thalorin has no army worth the name,” Elias said at last, when the courtyard had given way to a garden path along the river. “We survive by being necessary to people who do. It is a different kind of strength than your house practices. Less impressive to watch. Harder to burn down.”

“I had not thought of it as strength,” Lucian admitted.

“Most northerners don't, until they need a healer and discover their own swords are useless for that particular problem.” Elias glanced sideways at him, not unkindly. “You'll want to remember that Thalorin exists for reasons beyond your mother's marriage, when the time comes that it matters. It usually does, eventually.”

They did not finish the thought before Selene's voice reached them from farther down the bank, high and delighted, followed almost immediately by a splash.

Lucian was moving before he had fully decided to. He reached the water's edge to find Selene sitting waist-deep in the shallows, soaked through and laughing rather than distressed, a broken reed still clutched in one hand as evidence of whatever ambitious crossing attempt had ended this way. Ysara was already there ahead of him, wading in without any apparent concern for her own fine skirts, hauling her granddaughter up by both arms with the practiced ease of someone who had clearly done this before, for someone.

“I was reaching for a fish,” Selene explained, entirely unrepentant, water streaming from her hair.

“There are no fish that close to the bank,” Lucian said.

“I didn't know that until I reached.”

Ysara laughed again, wringing water from the girl's sleeve with brisk, familiar hands, and looked up to find Lucian still standing rigid at the water's edge, his hand having half-lifted toward the river before Ysara reached her.

“She does this at home too, I expect,” Ysara said, not quite a question.

“Yes.”

“And you go in after her.”

“Someone has to.”

Ysara studied him a moment longer than the exchange required, something behind her eyes doing a quiet, careful accounting of its own. “Yes,” she said finally. “I imagine someone always has, with you.”

She did not explain what she meant by that, and led Selene off toward the house to be changed before anyone could ask her to, leaving Lucian standing at the river's edge beside his uncle, the water running clear and unbothered over stones that had never needed to be defended.

That evening, after supper, Lucian found himself in the colder end of a warm hallway — a servant's error in the placement of a single unlit sconce, nothing more — near enough to the small solar where his mother sat with Ysara that their voices reached him before he decided whether to announce himself.

“He thanked her for reprimanding him,” Ysara was saying, quiet, not meant to travel. “At the river. Not resentment. Gratitude, as if he expected nothing less and was relieved to receive it.”

“He is careful,” Elara said. “He has always been careful.”

“He is twelve, Elara. He should not have to be careful about whether his sister is allowed to fall in a river.” A pause, softer. “I am not blaming you. I know what that house asks of him. I only wonder, sometimes, what is left over for him to keep.”

Elara's answer, when it came, was quieter still, quiet enough that Lucian caught only its shape and not its words, and he found that he did not want to hear the rest of it standing in a servant's corridor with cooling supper still on his tongue. He withdrew the way he had come, careful of his own footsteps for reasons he did not examine closely, and did not mention what he had heard to anyone, then or after.

They left on the seventh day, the carriage turning north again toward colder light and harder stone. Selene fell asleep within the hour, her head against Elara's shoulder, a river-smoothed pebble still clutched in one fist that no one had yet convinced her to surrender. Elara watched the green fade back toward grey with the same loosened stillness she had carried on the way down, though something in it had tightened again by degrees, the closer they came to home.

Lucian watched it too, and did not know, exactly, what he was watching for. Only that Ysara's question had lodged itself somewhere he had not given her permission to reach, and that he carried it north with the rest — unresolved, unnamed, and entirely his own to keep.
