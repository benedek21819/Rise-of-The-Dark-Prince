# Chapter 25: Blood

More than a year had passed since the springs, and Firsthold looked smaller to Lucian than it had the last time he'd walked its streets — not because the city had changed, but because he had.

He was fourteen now, tall enough that Cole no longer needed to shorten his stride to match him, and the descent from Blackspire no longer left his legs burning by the time the Ascend leveled into the valley. Dain had not come with them. That fact alone marked the day as different from every other visit to the city Lucian could remember — his father's quiet decision, delivered without ceremony a week prior, that the heir could be trusted to walk his own capital with an escort of two Silver Guard at a respectful distance and his sister at his side, rather than an instructor standing over both.

Lyra had dressed plainly for the descent, dark travel wool rather than anything finer, but plainness had stopped disguising much about her some time in the last year. She was nineteen now, and the city noticed her the way it had only recently learned to — glances held a half-second too long from stall-keepers and apprentices, quickly corrected once they registered who walked beside her.

“You're staring at the cheese merchant,” Lucian said.

“I am not staring. I am considering whether to buy cheese.”

“You considered it for the length of an entire street.”

“It's very good cheese.” She did not look at him, but her mouth had the particular set it took when she was fighting a smile and losing. “You've gotten difficult to tease. You used to just go quiet and let me win.”

“I still let you win.”

“You do not.”

They continued down through the market row, the ordinary noise of Firsthold closing around them — the ring of a smith's hammer somewhere off the main street, a cart's wheels catching on frost-cracked stone, the layered voices of vendors calling prices that dropped, without exception, the moment they recognized the black cloaks and the sigil at the throat. Lucian carried himself the way he always did in the city: watchful without appearing to watch, cataloguing faces and doorways and the particular rhythm of a street that was behaving as it should. Lyra walked beside him with an ease he had never quite managed to match, easy enough that he found himself, more than once, positioning half a step ahead of her without deciding to.

The three men were waiting near the mouth of a narrower lane where the market row thinned toward the tanners' quarter.

Lucian marked them before they moved — the stillness of men who were not shopping, not working, only waiting, arranged with just enough distance between them to not look arranged at all. Travel-worn clothes, southern cut, nothing overtly wrong except that nothing about them belonged to this particular street on this particular morning. He adjusted his path a fraction, angling to put more distance between them and Lyra without breaking stride, the way Dain had drilled into him a hundred times over — read the room before the room asks to be read.

It was not enough distance.

The tallest of the three peeled off first, unhurried, and set himself directly into their path with the loose, deliberate insolence of a man who had already decided what the encounter would cost him and found the price acceptable.

“Lady Caldarion,” he said, and the honorific came out wrong, worn smooth of respect until only the shape of it remained. “Fine morning for the market. Fine company you keep.”

“Move aside,” Lucian said. He did not raise his voice. He did not need to.

The man's eyes flicked to him and away again, dismissing him with the particular carelessness of someone who saw a boy rather than an heir. “I wasn't speaking to the boy.”

“You are speaking to House Caldarion,” Lyra said, level and cold in a register Lucian had rarely heard from her. “Which is the last mistake you will make today if you do not step aside now.”

The second man laughed, short and ugly, and said something low to the third that Lucian did not catch and did not need to. The tall one's gaze had already slid past the warning entirely, fixed on Lyra with an intent that had stopped being about the street or the cheese merchant or anything so small as insolence.

He reached for her.

Lucian moved before the reach completed, closing the half-step of distance he had tried and failed to create, but the man was already committed, his open hand rising toward Lyra's face in the same motion — and Lucian's blade was already clear of its sheath, the draw and the strike a single unbroken line the way Dain had drilled into him across a thousand cold mornings, no display, no wasted motion, only the exact geometry required to solve the problem in front of him.

The edge caught the man's wrist an inch below the raised hand.

The cut was clean and did not waste itself on more than it needed to accomplish. The hand did not complete its arc toward Lyra's face. It fell instead, along with the man attached to it, a short, strangled sound tearing out of him as he folded around the wound.

Firsthold moved before the hand finished falling.

A stall-keeper three paces off was already vaulting his own counter, a length of iron weighing-bar still in his fist. A watchman at the lane's mouth had his spear level before the wounded man's knees found the cobbles. An old woman who had been arguing over the price of onions a moment before was screaming a single word — *Guard!* — with a clarity that cut clean through the market noise, and the word traveled faster than any of them could run, taken up by voices Lucian could not see, relayed deeper into the city like a struck bell finding its echo. It was, Lucian understood even in the midst of it, exactly what Dain had told him the North's loyalty meant: not a song, but the instinctive violence of people who remembered what House Caldarion's absence would have cost them, converging on a threat to its blood before they had finished deciding to.

He did not have time to feel the weight of that. The second and third men were already moving — not toward him, not toward the wounded one, but sideways, fast, cutting into the gap between two stalls with the unhurried desperation of men who had rehearsed the exit before they ever needed it.

A cart went over in front of the nearest pursuers.

It should have been an accident — a startled draft-horse, an ordinary market mishap — except that Lucian, watching with the same cold attention he had turned on the three men from the first moment, saw the hand that struck the animal's flank to spook it, saw the deliberate angle of the fall that spilled crated goods directly into the watchman's path rather than away from it. A second man in a plain brown coat, who had been standing at the wounded man's back only a moment before and appeared to be helping now, hauled the third fleeing man bodily around a corner in a direction that was not the direction anyone pursuing would expect, shouting something that sent two well-meaning bystanders sprinting the wrong way entirely.

By the time the tangle of cart and crates and shouting cleared, the second and third men were gone. Only the one Lucian had cut remained, curled in the frost-slick street, surrounded now by a ring of Firsthold's own — the stall-keeper with his iron bar still raised, the watchman's spear resting an inch from the fallen man's throat, more converging by the second from every direction the alarm had reached.

Lucian stood very still, his blade still drawn, and did not sheath it yet.

Lyra's hand found his arm, gripping hard enough to hurt, and when he glanced at her she was pale but steady, already composing herself back into the shape the street expected of her.

“You are unhurt,” he said. It was not quite a question.

“Yes.” Her voice shook once, on the single word, and then did not shake again. “Lucian — the others—”

“I saw.”

He had. That was the part that sat coldest in him, colder than the wind, colder than the blood cooling on his blade. Three men did not simply happen to have a spooked horse and a false shout and a second escort ready to peel a fleeing man out of a crowd's reach in the same three seconds it took a wound to open. That was not opportunism. That was a plan, held in reserve, built by people who had known exactly what a crowd like this one would do the instant Caldarion blood was threatened — and had prepared, in advance, for precisely that reaction.

Someone had studied this street. Studied this city. Studied them.

The watchman was calling for order, for space, for someone to fetch the nearest Silver Guard post, and the crowd's noise rose and settled around the single wounded, captured man like water closing over a stone. Lucian looked down at him — at the plain, deliberately unremarkable clothes, the wound already being bound rough and ungently by hands with no gentleness left to spare him — and understood, with the same clarity he brought to a fault in a training form, that whatever answers this man carried would be small ones. The men who had built the escape had not left their most replaceable piece behind by accident.

He sheathed his blade at last, and turned to see Lyra safely surrounded by the two Silver Guard, who had arrived at a dead run and now stood between her and the thinning edges of the crowd with their glaives already lowered.

“It's over,” one of them said, to Lyra, in the tone reserved for reassuring the shaken.

Lucian did not correct him.

He only looked once more toward the mouth of the lane where two men had vanished into a city that was supposed to hold no room for anyone to vanish into, and understood, with a certainty that settled into him as cleanly as the cut had landed, that whatever this morning had been, it was not the end of anything.

It was the first thing he had been allowed to see of something much larger, still moving, somewhere beneath the streets he had thought he knew.
