# Chapter 76: Mark

He went down to the city two days after the letters went south, and took his brothers with him.

Nobody had suggested it. A steward came up at the second hour wanting to know whether grain should be sent down to the second gate or the people at the gate brought up to the granary, and Lucian opened his mouth to answer and found that he had no idea what the second gate looked like with people living in it.

So he went to see.

---

They met the courier coming up.

He was a Crown rider, southern, mud to the thigh, walking on legs that had done three days in the saddle and then the Ascend. He came through the gate arch and saw the three of them standing in it and got his back straight faster than was strictly necessary.

"From Aurelia, my lord."

"How were the passes."

"Closing." The man was fumbling at the bag. "Two days more, maybe three, and the eastern one is gone until Thaw."

There was one letter in it. Lucian knew the hand before it was all the way out of the bag — the loops too big, the way she had written since she was nine and had never troubled to grow out of.

He looked at it for perhaps as long as it takes to read a name.

Then he put it inside his coat and told the man to go up and get fed.

Riven watched the coat. He did not say anything, which cost him something.

---

The second gate had people in it.

They were in the gatehouse and along the inside of the wall and in the lee of the barbican, under sailcloth and cart-covers and one enormous striped awning that had certainly come off a merchant's stall, and there was washing strung between the arrow slits. Somebody had built a cook fire in the guard alcove, which was against every standing order Blackspire had, and had swept the ash back neatly against the wall.

Nine days ago Lucian had told a man to open this gate and had thought about it for perhaps four seconds.

Just inside the arch, against the wall, there was a stall with a plank counter worn into a dip at one end.

There was a woman behind it now, selling nothing very much — thread, tallow, a basket of the small hard winter apples that keep. She had her own goods laid out on somebody else's plank.

"That was Mother Cabb's," Riven said.

"I know."

"She would not go. Sennet told me. They sent a cart up for her three times and the third time she swore at the driver in front of about forty people."

"I know," Lucian said. "Dain read it out."

Riven looked at the stall a moment longer.

"Somebody should tell that woman whose plank that is."

"Somebody will," Lucian said, and went through the arch and down.

---

He had seen Firsthold from four thousand feet with it burning. He had not walked in it since the autumn.

The Ascend came down into the upper market, and the upper market was standing, and that was the last thing that was. Past the fountain the market row began, and the market row was gone — not knocked down, burned to the cellars, so that what was left was a long open scar of black stone foundations with the shape of every house still legible in it, doorsteps and hearths and here and there a chimney standing up out of nothing.

It was cold. The fires had been out for four days and the ash was wet and there was still, in three or four places, a thin blue smoke coming up out of the ground where something underneath was going on burning.

There were perhaps two hundred people working in it. Digging out cellars, mostly, with the sort of tools you use for anything.

They were still finding bodies. Lucian had been told that and had written it down and had thought he understood it, and then a man near the middle of the row straightened up and called something over his shoulder and four others came at a walk with a door, and it took Lucian a moment to understand that the door was what they had to carry people on.

"Do we go down there," Riven said.

"Yes."

---

Riven talked.

He talked to the man with the door, and to a woman scraping a hearth out with a shovel, and to two boys sitting on the sill of a house that was not there any more, and inside twenty minutes he had names for all of them and their trades and where they were sleeping, and had passed on the names to Lucian in an undertone as they went, without once making a performance of it.

He had always been able to do that. Lucian had spent years being faintly irritated by it and had stopped, somewhere in the last month, being irritated.

Thorne did not talk at all.

But at the third cellar he put his coat on a stone and went down into the hole with the men, and stayed in it for an hour and a half, and came up filthy to the shoulder with his hands cut in three places. Nobody down there knew who he was. Somebody handed him the end of a beam and he took it.

Lucian watched him go down and understood not to say anything about it.

---

The tanners' quarter was worse, and smelled worse, and there was less of it.

Beyond it, where the engines had reached the eastern wall, the damage stopped being fire and started being masonry — a hundred and fifty feet of the outer face knocked into the ditch, and men on it in the cold with baskets, and the great stones stacked in the road.

A master mason walked him along the whole of it and explained, at some length and with real feeling, why the wall had failed where it had failed, which was not the engines but a repair done badly under Lucian's great-grandfather. He was extremely angry about the repair. He was not angry about anything else.

"It will want two seasons, my lord. Three if the frost is hard."

"Take three."

"There will be a question of whether it comes out of the city or the house."

"The house," Lucian said, and the man wrote it in a wax book and moved on to the next thing he was angry about.

---

What Lucian had not prepared for was being thanked.

It started at the wall and it did not stop. The mason did it. A carter did it. An old man on the market row took hold of his sleeve with both hands and told him that his daughter and his daughter's children had gone up the deep valley road on the second day and were alive in a barn at Ostmere, and thanked him, and then stood holding the sleeve and not saying anything for a while, and Lucian stood and let him.

Two women thanked him for the ditch parties. A boy of about eleven wanted to know whether the black one had killed the other one himself or whether the third one had, and was told, and went away entirely satisfied.

Nobody said anything else. Nobody looked at him and thought about the count. Half of them looked at the stitched side of his face and looked away from it politely, and thanked him.

Eleven hundred and some of them were dead.

By the middle of the afternoon Lucian had stopped being able to answer people in whole sentences, and Riven noticed, and began answering them for him.

---

They came back up the Ascend in the last of the light.

He went up to the roost before anything else. The keepers had the flank open again and were arguing quietly about the linen, and Ancalion had shifted perhaps a foot on the grit since the morning, which the head keeper reported as though it were a considerable event, and which was the best news anybody had given him in four days.

---

His own room, late, with the door shut and one candle and his boots still on.

Three pages, and the loops too big.

It had snowed in Aurelia and stopped, which she reported as a personal grievance. She had been given a horse she liked and a hound she did not. She had found two places where a book out of the Hall contradicted itself and had set them both out for him at length, and wanted to know whether the northern harbour was in fact silted or whether the man had made it up.

She asked after Ancalion. She asked whether the snow had come north yet.

At the bottom of the third page, in the same untroubled hand, without any preamble at all:

*You wrote that you intend to be better. I am not going to ask you what you meant, because you would explain it and then it would be smaller.*

*I will simply hold you to it.*

He read it twice.

She would have his own letter tomorrow, or the day after.
