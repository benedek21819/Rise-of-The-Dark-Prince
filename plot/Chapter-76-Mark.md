# Chapter 76: Mark

He went down to the city two days after the letters went south, and took his brothers with him.

Nobody had suggested it. He had spent the morning finding reasons not to, and had run out of them somewhere around the point where a steward asked him whether the lower granary should be opened to the second gate or the second gate brought up to the granary, and he had discovered that he did not know what the second gate looked like with people living in it.

So he went to see.

---

They met the courier coming up.

He was a Crown rider, southern, with his horse blown and mud to the knee and the look of a man who has spent three days making a three-day road. He came through the gate arch and saw three of them standing in it and got down off his horse faster than was strictly necessary.

"From Aurelia, my lord."

"How were the passes."

"Closing." The man was fumbling at the bag. "Two days more, maybe three, and the eastern one is gone until Thaw."

There was one letter in it. Lucian knew the hand before it was all the way out of the bag — the loops too big, the way she had written since she was nine and had never troubled to grow out of.

He looked at it for perhaps as long as it takes to read a name.

Then he put it inside his coat and told the man to go up and get fed.

Riven watched the coat. He did not say anything, which cost him something.

---

The second gate had people in it.

They were in the gatehouse and along the inside of the wall and in the lee of the barbican, under sailcloth and cart-covers and one enormous striped awning that had certainly come off a merchant's stall, and there was washing strung between the arrow slits. Somebody had built a cook fire in the guard alcove, which was against every standing order Blackspire had, and had swept the ash back neatly against the wall.

Nine days ago Lucian had told a man to open this gate and had thought about it for perhaps four seconds.

Just inside the arch, against the wall, there was a stall with a plank counter worn into a dip at one end.

There was a woman behind it now, selling nothing very much — thread, tallow, a basket of the small hard winter apples that keep. She had her own goods laid out on somebody else's plank.

"That was Mother Cabb's," Riven said.

"I know."

"She would not go. Sennet told me. They sent a cart up for her three times and the third time she swore at the driver in front of about forty people."

"I know," Lucian said. "Dain read it out."

Riven looked at the stall a moment longer.

"Somebody should tell that woman whose plank that is."

"Somebody will," Lucian said, and went through the arch and down.

---

He had seen Firsthold from four thousand feet with it burning. He had not walked in it since the autumn.

The Ascend came down into the upper market, and the upper market was standing, and that was the last thing that was. Past the fountain the market row began, and the market row was gone — not knocked down, burned to the cellars, so that what was left was a long open scar of black stone foundations with the shape of every house still legible in it, doorsteps and hearths and here and there a chimney standing up out of nothing.

It was cold. The fires had been out for four days and the ash was wet and there was still, in three or four places, a thin blue smoke coming up out of the ground where something underneath was going on burning.

There were perhaps two hundred people working in it. Digging out cellars, mostly, with the sort of tools you use for anything.

They were still finding bodies. Lucian had been told that and had written it down and had thought he understood it, and then a man near the middle of the row straightened up and called something over his shoulder and four others came at a walk with a door, and it took Lucian a moment to understand that the door was what they had to carry people on.

"Do we go down there," Riven said.

"Yes."

---

Riven talked.

He talked to the man with the door, and to a woman scraping a hearth out with a shovel, and to two boys sitting on the sill of a house that was not there any more, and inside twenty minutes he had names for all of them and their trades and where they were sleeping, and had passed on the names to Lucian in an undertone as they went, without once making a performance of it.

He had always been able to do that. Lucian had spent years being faintly irritated by it and had stopped, somewhere in the last month, being irritated.

Thorne did not talk at all.

But at the third cellar he put his coat on a stone and went down into the hole with the men, and stayed in it for an hour and a half, and came up filthy to the shoulder with his hands cut in three places. Nobody down there knew who he was. Somebody handed him the end of a beam and he took it.

Lucian watched him go down and understood not to say anything about it.

---

The tanners' quarter was worse, and smelled worse, and there was less of it.

Beyond it, where the engines had reached the eastern wall, the damage stopped being fire and started being masonry — a hundred and fifty feet of the outer face knocked into the ditch, and men on it in the cold with baskets, and the great stones stacked in the road.

A master mason walked him along the whole of it and explained, at some length and with real feeling, why the wall had failed where it had failed, which was not the engines but a repair done badly under Lucian's great-grandfather. He was extremely angry about the repair. He was not angry about anything else.

"It will want two seasons, my lord. Three if the frost is hard."

"Take three."

"There will be a question of whether it comes out of the city or the house."

"The house," Lucian said, and the man wrote it in a wax book and moved on to the next thing he was angry about.

---

What Lucian had not prepared for was being thanked.

It started at the wall and it did not stop. The mason did it. A carter did it. An old man on the market row took hold of his sleeve with both hands and told him that his daughter and his daughter's children had gone up the deep valley road on the second day and were alive in a barn at Ostmere, and thanked him, and then stood holding the sleeve and not saying anything for a while, and Lucian stood and let him.

Two women thanked him for the ditch parties. A boy of about eleven wanted to know whether the black one had killed the other one himself or whether the third one had, and was told, and went away entirely satisfied.

Nobody said anything else. Nobody looked at him and thought about the count. Half of them looked at the stitched side of his face and looked away from it politely, and thanked him.

Eleven hundred and some of them were dead.

By the middle of the afternoon Lucian had stopped being able to answer people in whole sentences, and Riven noticed, and began answering them for him.

---

They came back up the Ascend in the last of the light, and there was a man of the household waiting at the second gate.

"My lord. Your father asks for you when you are back."

"Now?"

"He said when you were back," the man said, "and he said not to let you go up to the roost first."

---

Vesper was in the small room off the long gallery with the shutters closed and four candles on the table, which was more light than he ever used.

He had come up off the scree the night before. It showed. But he was shaved and in a clean coat and sitting straight, which he had not been doing two days ago on a rock.

On the table in front of him was a glass sphere about the size of a large apple, on a folded cloth, with the cut ends of a harness strap still buckled to the collar of it.

"Sit down," Vesper said. "You are filthy."

"I have been in the city."

"I know where you have been. Sit down anyway."

Lucian sat.

"I have had this since the morning after," Vesper said. "I have spent most of two days on it and I have got as far as I am going to get without somebody who knows more than I do, and I am not going to ask anybody who knows more than I do."

He turned it a quarter turn on the cloth with one finger. Inside the glass there was nothing at all to see — no core, no working, no cloud. It was a ball of glass.

"It is Myrath's."

Lucian did not say anything for a moment.

"You are certain."

"Nobody else on this continent can lay four workings into one object and have them run together without tearing. Two, possibly. Not four." Vesper turned it back again. "And it is made the way they make things. It is not a trace and there is nothing to prove; it is a mark. You would know a Myrath piece the way you know a Blackspire blade at ten feet, and I have handled enough of both."

"Does it still work?"

"Yes. Not as it worked coming across the North — that took nearly everything it had, and it will never do that again. But it is not spent." He looked up. "It would still cover a man. It would cover this table."

Lucian looked at the glass for a while.

"Roderic told me where it was," he said. "He did not tell me who made it."

"He did not have to. He knew exactly what he was handing you." Vesper sat back. "He gave you the one thing in his possession that would cost somebody else something, and he did it two hours before he found out he was going to live. That is not a broken man. Remember it when you go south."

---

"The letter to the King went two days ago."

"It did."

"With nothing about this in it."

"Because I had not finished with it, and I do not write things I am not finished with." Vesper folded his hands. "There is a second letter to be written or there is not. What would you do?"

Lucian had been expecting something else, and had to stop and put it down.

"Send it," he said. "Or send word of it. Ilyandra Myrath sold an object that walked forty thousand men across the North without one dragon in this house feeling them come. Nine hundred and forty people burned in that city. She should have to stand somewhere and say out loud that she did not ask what it was for."

"Yes," Vesper said. "And?"

"And there is no charge in it." He heard himself say it and did not much like it. "She sold a thing to a lord who paid for it. She was never told. It is not treason and there is nothing in law to hold her with, and she will say all of that in front of the court and every word of it will be true."

"So the Crown does what."

"Nothing. Regret. A payment, perhaps."

"And the object?"

Lucian stopped.

"Goes to the Crown," he said slowly. "As evidence. And stays there."

"It goes into a case in Aurelia," Vesper agreed, "and half the continent knows this house had it and gave it up, and Myrath is publicly embarrassed for one season and then it is finished, and everybody's accounts are square." He let that sit exactly long enough. "And a house that has paid is a house that owes you nothing."

The candles did their work. Outside, a long way down, somebody was shouting on the lower court about a cart.

"Corvane," Lucian said.

"Corvane." Vesper did not look pleased about being understood; he looked like a man ticking something off. "Your grandfather caught them running men into this house for eleven years and did not break them. He let them keep their seat and their name and their trade, and he never once told them what he knew, and there has not been a Corvane in forty years who could sit down in a room with a Caldarion and be comfortable. That is worth more than a ruined house. A ruined house is finished. A house that owes you is a thing you have."

"So we do nothing."

"We hold it," Vesper said. "That is not nothing. That is the whole of it."

"And they never find out whether we will use it."

"They never find out. That is the price, and they will pay it for as long as the house stands." He put one finger on the cloth beside the glass. "Somebody will tell them we have it — a clerk, a rider, one of Roderic's officers walking home with a story. They will hear this winter. And then they will wait, and nothing will come, and they will wait through the trial and nothing will come, and by the spring after next Ilyandra Myrath will have decided she was never in any danger, and she will still not be able to look at our banner without checking herself."

He said it perfectly evenly and it was, Lucian thought, the coldest thing he had ever heard his father say.

"I do not like it."

"No." Vesper wrapped the cloth over the glass, once, and then again. "You are not required to. You are required to be able to do it."

---

His own room, late, with the door shut and one candle and his boots still on.

Three pages, and the loops too big.

It had snowed in Aurelia and stopped, which she reported as a personal grievance. Her tutor had been replaced by a man who read aloud with expression and she did not know how much longer she could be expected to bear it. She had been given a horse she liked and a hound she did not. She had read something out of the Hall about the founding of the northern port and had found two places where it contradicted itself, and had set them out for him at some length and with evident satisfaction, and wanted to know whether the harbour was in fact silted or whether the man had made that up.

She asked after Ancalion. She asked whether the snow had come north yet.

At the bottom of the third page, in the same untroubled hand, without any preamble at all:

*You wrote that you intend to be better. I am not going to ask you what you meant, because you would explain it and then it would be smaller.*

*I will simply hold you to it.*

Lucian read that twice and then set the pages down flat on the table.

There was no answer to make. The answer had gone south two days ago in a courier's bag — the siege and the forty thousand and Lirath brought down out of the sky and the stitches in his face — and by now it was somewhere past the Threefold, and her rider and his rider had passed each other in the dark on the same road without either of them knowing it.

She would have it tomorrow. Perhaps the day after.

He folded the three pages and put them in the drawer with the others, and blew out the candle, and went back up to the roost to sleep on the grit beside his dragon.
