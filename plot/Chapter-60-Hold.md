# Chapter 60: Hold

The training yard's sand had seen a great many mornings since the one where Thorne first blinked through a fraction of a second of nothing, and Lucian had spent nearly all of them coaxing that same door open again and again, each time a little longer than the last — a heartbeat, then two, then something closer to a full breath — the progress so incremental from one morning to the next that it had taken months, laid end to end, for him to fully trust it was progress at all rather than simple inconsistency.

"You have the look of a man about to ask us to hit him harder again," Riven said, rolling his shoulder with the particular resigned good humor of someone who had said some version of that sentence more mornings than he cared to count by now.

"I was going to phrase it more diplomatically."

"You never do." Thorne fell into his usual wide, patient circle, unhurried the way he always was before Riven's own restlessness forced the pace of anything. "Diplomacy has never once been how you ask either of us for a favor."

Dain stood at the yard's edge in his usual place, arms folded, and said nothing yet, content, as he always was now, to let the morning's first exchanges tell him what shape the lesson wanted to take before he named it aloud.

They had done this often enough by now that the twins no longer needed reminding what to bring to it — Riven's forcing aggression, Thorne's patient closing pressure, both of them pushing past the ordinary caution of a friendly bout into something closer to the real, unguarded urgency that seemed, more often than not, to be the only key that had ever once turned this particular lock. Lucian met the first rush the way he had learned to meet it, giving ground only exactly as much as the sword in his hand told him was safe to give, and felt, somewhere in the compressed middle of the second exchange, the same familiar door begin to shift beneath the pressure of it.

It did not arrive all at once this time. It arrived the way all the long, slow accumulation of failed mornings before it had arrived — by increments too small to notice individually, each one adding some fraction of a second to whatever had come before it, until the sum of them finally crossed some threshold neither Lucian nor anyone watching him had been able to name in advance.

Thorne's blade faltered mid-strike, exactly as it had a hundred mornings before this one, his eyes losing their certainty of where the ground and the world around it actually stood. But this time it did not pass after a breath or two the way it usually did. It held — one breath, then a second, then a third — long enough that Riven, pressing in from the flank a half-step behind his brother, stumbled through the same sudden absence and very nearly overbalanced into Thorne's own faltering guard.

Lucian felt it the way he had felt it countless times by now, except stretched further than it had ever once gone before, long enough to actually study its own shape rather than simply confirm, yet again, that it was still there to be found — his own sight and hearing sharpened past anything he had ever managed to summon at will, every grain of shifting sand and every catch of breath in the yard laid out before him with a clarity that had nothing ordinary left in it, while both his brothers stood inside a silence and a blindness neither of them had any way to fight through. He held it, not fully understanding yet how he was holding it, only aware that some part of him had stopped straining against the door and simply let it stay open instead of forcing it wider.

It broke, finally, on its own, several full seconds after it had begun, and both twins staggered half a step in the sudden return of their own senses, blinking hard against a world that had simply resumed as though it had never once left them.

"That," Thorne said, sitting down rather more abruptly than he intended to, "was considerably longer than the last time."

"Considerably," Riven agreed, still shaking his head slightly, an expression on his face that had moved past his usual easy alarm into something closer to genuine unsettlement. "I would like it noted, again, that I remain unenthusiastic about this particular exercise, whatever it apparently teaches you."

Dain crossed the yard without hurrying, and studied both twins with the same clinical care he always gave them afterward, finding nothing this time either — no lasting harm, only two young men shaking off several seconds of a world gone abruptly absent rather than the fraction of one. He looked at Lucian for a long moment before he spoke.

"How long."

"I do not know precisely. Longer than it has ever held before. I could feel it start to slip near the end, the way water slips through a hand no longer gripping it tightly enough." Lucian turned the sensation over as he said it, still not fully certain he had the right words for something no one in this house's history had ever needed words for before. "It did not feel like I found something new today. It felt like everything these past months have already taught me finally added up to enough."

"That is very likely exactly what happened." Something in Dain's face carried none of the easy, immediate praise he sometimes allowed after an ordinary clean bout — instead a slower, more considered weight, the particular respect he reserved for progress that had cost real time rather than arriving as a single stroke of fortune. "I told you, some time ago, that a fraction of a second would most mornings have to be enough. It has grown a little further almost every week since, by my own count, but never by this much in a single morning. That is worth naming plainly rather than simply folding into the ordinary pace of things."

"It is still indiscriminate," Lucian said. "I could not spare either of them from it. I do not know yet whether I ever will be able to."

"No. You could not, and you may not, for longer still than either of us would prefer." Dain's tone did not soften the fact of it, though something almost warm sat beneath the bluntness. "But you held a door open for several seconds today that would not stay open for the length of a single heartbeat when this began. Control of what passes through it is a different mountain entirely, and I will not pretend to you that this one gets you any closer to its base than you already stood. But the door itself is no longer merely something that happens to you. You are beginning to be the one who decides when it opens."

Riven, still sitting in the sand with the particular grim humor of a man who had made his peace with an unpleasant but apparently necessary role in someone else's training, looked between Lucian and Thorne with theatrical resignation. "I would like the record to reflect that I am owed something considerable for this. A decent meal, at minimum. Possibly an apology written in advance for whatever comes next, when three seconds becomes thirty."

"Duly noted," Lucian said, and found, helping his brother up from the sand, that the smile that came with it was neither small nor tired this time. "Firsthold, then. Today, if Dain can spare us."

Riven blinked at him, whatever reply he had been readying visibly abandoned. "You are agreeing with me. Deliberately. Out loud."

"You have spent the better part of a year letting me practice losing my senses on the two of you without once refusing me. I am capable of noticing when a debt has actually come due." Lucian looked to Dain, who had already begun, with the particular unhurried economy of a man who had half expected the request before it arrived, arranging the shape of the rest of the morning around it. "If it can be spared."

"It can." Dain's approval carried none of the reluctance either twin seemed braced for. "I will have two men from the garrison meet you at the lower gate rather than send you down entirely on your own account — that much is not a request, whatever else about this morning is. Firsthold on an ordinary day asks very little of a Caldarion escort. I see no reason to test whether today is ordinary by skipping it."

The descent felt shorter than Lucian remembered it from childhood, the Ascend's long fortified turns familiar enough now that his feet found their footing without asking his attention for it, and by the time the valley opened out beneath them Riven had already resumed the argument about whether a hawk raised this close to a dragon roost ought to be fiercer than an ordinary hawk, apparently unwilling to let too many months pass without eventually returning to it.

Firsthold at midday moved the way it always did — market stalls crowded three deep along the main thoroughfare, the smell of woodsmoke and roasting meat threading through the colder mountain air, the particular unhurried bustle of a city that had never once needed to fear an enemy reaching it. Lucian bought them all a meal from a stall Dain had pointed them toward without quite admitting he had a favorite among the valley's cooks, and the three of them ate standing at the edge of the square, close enough to the garrison escort's discreet watch that no one troubled them and far enough from it that no one watching would have guessed, from the easy noise of it, that they were anything more than three brothers enjoying an ordinary afternoon.

"I do not actually mind it," Thorne said eventually, quieter than his brother's usual volume, turning his own cup slowly between his hands. "The training. Losing myself to it for a few seconds at a time. I know Riven complains for both of us, and I let him, because it is easier than admitting I do not share the complaint as fully as he does."

"Why not."

"Because it means something, watching you get better at it. Not just for you — for what it will mean someday, for all of us, when whatever this is finally answers to your will instead of only to danger." Thorne's gaze stayed steady on him, none of his usual mildness dulling the honesty of it. "You do not ask us for very much, Lucian. Of all the things you could have asked two brothers to give you, a few seconds of blindness in a training yard is not a large price, however loudly Riven insists on charging you for it."

"I insist on charging him for it," Riven said, entirely unbothered by being spoken of in the third person, "because someone in this family ought to be keeping an honest accounting of what we are each owed, and it is clearly not going to be either of you."

"You keep the accounting," Lucian said, "and I will keep paying it, because I find, more and more lately, that I would rather owe the two of you something honestly than owe you nothing and have neither of you close enough to collect it."

Riven opened his mouth for what was plainly building toward another joke, caught something in Lucian's face that was not quite joking itself, and let it go instead, settling for reaching over and closing the last of Lucian's own meal firmly into his own hand. "Then you can start by not finishing this. Some debts are better paid in bread."

"That is not remotely how debts work."

"It is exactly how they work, in this family, and you know it." Thorne's mouth curved, quiet and genuine, and for a while none of them said anything more that needed saying, three brothers standing in an ordinary square in an ordinary city, for once asking nothing further of each other than the plain, uncomplicated pleasure of the afternoon itself.
