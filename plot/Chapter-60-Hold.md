# Chapter 60: Hold

The training yard's sand had seen a great many mornings since the one where Thorne first blinked through a fraction of a second of nothing, and Lucian had spent nearly all of them chasing the same brief, stubborn door without once managing to hold it open longer than that first accidental heartbeat had.

"You have the look of a man about to ask us to hit him harder again," Riven said, rolling his shoulder with the particular resigned good humor of someone who had said some version of that sentence more mornings than he cared to count this past season.

"I was going to phrase it more diplomatically."

"You never do." Thorne fell into his usual wide, patient circle, unhurried the way he always was before Riven's own restlessness forced the pace of anything. "Diplomacy has never once been how you ask either of us for a favor."

Dain stood at the yard's edge in his usual place, arms folded, and said nothing yet, content, as he always was now, to let the morning's first exchanges tell him what shape the lesson wanted to take before he named it aloud.

They had done this often enough across the season that the twins no longer needed reminding what to bring to it — Riven's forcing aggression, Thorne's patient closing pressure, both of them pushing past the ordinary caution of a friendly bout into something closer to the real, unguarded urgency that seemed, more often than not, to be the only key that had ever once turned this particular lock. Lucian met the first rush the way he had learned to meet it, giving ground only exactly as much as the sword in his hand told him was safe to give, and felt, somewhere in the compressed middle of the second exchange, the same familiar door begin to shift beneath the pressure of it.

It did not arrive all at once this time. It arrived the way the season's whole slow accumulation of failed mornings had arrived — by increments too small to notice individually, each one adding some fraction of a second to whatever had come before it, until the sum of them finally crossed some threshold neither Lucian nor anyone watching him had been able to name in advance.

Thorne's blade faltered mid-strike, exactly as it had a season ago, his eyes losing their certainty of where the ground and the world around it actually stood. But this time it did not pass after a single heartbeat. It held — one breath, then a second, then a third — long enough that Riven, pressing in from the flank a half-step behind his brother, stumbled through the same sudden absence and very nearly overbalanced into Thorne's own faltering guard.

Lucian felt it the way he had felt it only once before, except stretched now across a span of time long enough to actually notice its own shape rather than simply confirm its existence — his own sight and hearing sharpened past anything he had ever managed to summon at will, every grain of shifting sand and every catch of breath in the yard laid out before him with a clarity that had nothing ordinary left in it, while both his brothers stood inside a silence and a blindness neither of them had any way to fight through. He held it, not fully understanding yet how he was holding it, only aware that some part of him had stopped straining against the door and simply let it stay open instead of forcing it wider.

It broke, finally, on its own, several full seconds after it had begun, and both twins staggered half a step in the sudden return of their own senses, blinking hard against a world that had simply resumed as though it had never once left them.

"That," Thorne said, sitting down rather more abruptly than he intended to, "was considerably longer than the last time."

"Considerably," Riven agreed, still shaking his head slightly, an expression on his face that had moved past his usual easy alarm into something closer to genuine unsettlement. "I would like it noted, again, that I remain unenthusiastic about this particular exercise, whatever it apparently teaches you."

Dain crossed the yard without hurrying, and studied both twins with the same clinical care he always gave them afterward, finding nothing this time either — no lasting harm, only two young men shaking off several seconds of a world gone abruptly absent rather than the fraction of one. He looked at Lucian for a long moment before he spoke.

"How long."

"I do not know precisely. Longer than it has ever held before. I could feel it start to slip near the end, the way water slips through a hand no longer gripping it tightly enough." Lucian turned the sensation over as he said it, still not fully certain he had the right words for something no one in this house's history had ever needed words for before. "It did not feel like I found something new today. It felt like everything this season has already taught me finally added up to enough."

"That is very likely exactly what happened." Something in Dain's face carried none of the easy, immediate praise he sometimes allowed after an ordinary clean bout — instead a slower, more considered weight, the particular respect he reserved for progress that had cost real time rather than arriving as a single stroke of fortune. "I told you, a season ago, that most mornings a fraction of a second would have to be enough. I did not expect it to still be true for quite this long, and I am glad, for both your sake and mine, that it is no longer true today."

"It is still indiscriminate," Lucian said. "I could not spare either of them from it. I do not know yet whether I ever will be able to."

"No. You could not, and you may not, for longer still than either of us would prefer." Dain's tone did not soften the fact of it, though something almost warm sat beneath the bluntness. "But you held a door open for several seconds today that would not stay open for the length of a single heartbeat a season ago. Control of what passes through it is a different mountain entirely, and I will not pretend to you that this one gets you any closer to its base than you already stood. But the door itself is no longer merely something that happens to you. You are beginning to be the one who decides when it opens."

Riven, still sitting in the sand with the particular grim humor of a man who had made his peace with an unpleasant but apparently necessary role in someone else's training, looked between Lucian and Thorne with theatrical resignation. "I would like the record to reflect that I am owed something considerable for this. Wine, at minimum. Possibly an apology written in advance for whatever comes next season, when three seconds becomes thirty."

"Duly noted," Lucian said, and found, helping his brother up from the sand, that the smile that came with it was neither small nor tired this time.
