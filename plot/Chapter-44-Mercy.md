# Chapter 44: Mercy

Lucian did not sleep well that night, and did not expect to.

He turned it over slowly, refusing to let the shape of it settle before he had examined every edge. Destruction was the easiest answer, and the one his own anger, still raw from the crossing, argued for loudest in the small hours — Corvane erased entirely, its name struck from the Duchy's record, a clean and total ending that would cost him nothing further to carry. Mercy was easier still, in its own quieter way, the kind of answer that let him tell himself afterward that he had chosen the gentler path without ever asking whether gentleness was actually owed here, or merely comfortable to extend. Neither one, turned over long enough, survived its own weight. He kept returning, instead, to the tenants he had never met and never would, people who had held no vote in Aldous Corvane's fear and would pay for it regardless if the whole house was made to answer as one. By the time the grey northern dawn came up over the eastern wall, he understood he had not been searching for the correct answer so much as making certain he was not choosing the easy one simply because it required less of him.

He found his father in the solar before Vesper had sent for him, which seemed to satisfy something in Vesper's expression before Lucian had said a single word.

"House Corvane's authority ends," Lucian said. "Lord Aldous will answer personally for what was done under his banner, whether or not the choice was ever truly his to make — a lord who cannot govern his own house is no longer fit to hold it, regardless of the reason. His line's claim to the lands is dissolved. But the people under that banner — the tenants, the miners, the men and women who never saw a coin of Dravenhall's money and never chose a war they were given no voice in — they are not Corvane's crime to inherit. Their lands come under Blackspire's own administration, directly, the same as any holding without a lord to answer for it. Not handed to Harrow as a prize. Not left to rot as a warning to the rest of the Duchy."

Vesper was quiet for a long moment, and when he spoke there was something in his voice Lucian had not heard directed at him in exactly that shape before — not approval exactly, though it was close to that, and not simple relief, though there was some of that too. "You did not destroy them, and you did not forgive them. You found the ground between the two mistakes I have made in this house's name, one or the other, more times than I care to count." He inclined his head, once, the closest thing to a bow Lucian had ever seen his father offer anyone. "It is done. I will see the writ drawn today."

They rode out to Harrow's border a final time three days later, a smaller company than either the parley or the ambush had required, and found Osric waiting at the same scorched ground where the first meeting had taken place — though the way-station behind him had begun, in the weeks since, to show the first raw timber of rebuilding rather than only ash.

"It is finished, Steward Osric," Lucian told him. "The claim is no longer disputed. Corvane's banner will not trouble your people again, and the lands beyond your own border now answer directly to Blackspire, not to any lord who might one day inherit the same grudge."

Osric's bow was lower and steadier than the one he had given eight days into his grief, weeks before. "Harrow will not forget this, my lord. Neither the burning, nor who came to end it."

"Do not let the remembering become the whole of what this place is, Steward Osric. A people who build their future entirely out of what was done to them rarely build anything worth living in afterward." Lucian glanced past him, to the raw timber rising where the way-station's ash had been. "Grieve what was lost. I would not ask otherwise of you. But let the new roof matter more, in the end, than the old fire that took the last one."

"That is easier counsel to give than to follow, my lord, though I do not say that as a complaint." Something almost wry crossed Osric's tired face. "We will try regardless. It is, I think, the only honest answer I can give you today."

"See that you build faster than you remember," Lucian said, not unkindly, and turned his horse back toward the column, the company falling in behind him for the ride home.

Blackspire received them back into a stretch of quiet that felt, after the crossing and the cells and the writing of a verdict Lucian would carry the rest of his life, almost unfamiliar in its stillness. A week passed in ordinary things — reports, small correspondence, an evening where Selene dragged him up to the eastern wall to check on Talon's now-fledged chicks without any errand attached to it at all.

"You are doing the quiet thing again," she informed him, watching the chicks rather than him, as though the observation cost her nothing to make while her attention was elsewhere. "Where you are standing right next to someone and not actually here at all."

"I am here."

"You are here the way the mountain is here. Very large, very still, and not paying attention to anything smaller than itself." She glanced at him sidelong, some of her usual teasing softened by something more careful underneath it. "Was it very bad. Whatever happened at Harrow."

"It was necessary. That is not always the same thing as bad, though it does not always feel very different from it either." Lucian watched the chicks settle for the night beside their mother, small and undramatic against the vast northern sky. "I do not want you carrying it, Selene. It is not yours to carry."

"I am not asking so I can carry it. I am asking because you look like someone who has been carrying it alone for too many days already, and I thought perhaps you would rather not, tonight, if only for an hour." She said it plainly, without any of her usual performance, and something in Lucian eased slightly, watching her watch the birds instead of him, exactly as she had promised.

A few more days passed in the same ordinary rhythm before Vesper sent for him again, this time not to the solar, but to the old armory beneath the keep's eastern tower, a room Lucian had passed a hundred times and entered perhaps three.

A single sword hung alone on the far wall, black and silver and unadorned, in a room otherwise given over to older, plainer steel.

"I have carried this sword for over twenty years," Vesper said, without turning from it at first, "and I could count on one hand the number of times I have drawn it in that span. Not because it is not a fine blade. Because it was never truly built for the way I fight." He turned then, and something in his face had gone unusually open, the particular honesty Lucian had come to associate only with the quietest rooms in this house. "I lean almost entirely on the Veil now, Lucian — on Weight, when it must be used at all, and on presence the rest of the time. A blade in my hand is, more often than not, a formality I keep because a lord is expected to carry one, not because it is where my strength truly lives."

He lifted it from the wall, and the black steel caught the low light in slow, moving lines, like something in it were still deciding whether to answer.

"You are not walking that path. You are walking both at once — the Still Blade in your hands and the Veil beneath it, holding to both in a way I never did and never will, and a sword built from Cold Silver and dragon bone was made for exactly the kind of hands that can use the whole of it, not only half. I would be lying to both of us if I told you this was only about what you did at that crossing, or what you chose to do afterward in the cells below this keep, or the harder thing you chose about Corvane three days past. It is about all of that, and it is also, plainly, the truth that I have very little further use for a sword you will use better than I ever have."

He held it out, hilt first, and Lucian took it into both hands, and felt, beneath the plain physical weight of good steel, something else entirely — a faint, deep resonance that seemed to settle rather than announce itself, quiet the way everything about this house's true strength had always been quiet.

"Does it have a name?" Lucian asked.

"Mercy," Vesper said, watching him hold it. "Not because we are a gentle house. Because the ones before you understood that true strength includes knowing how to end a thing cleanly, rather than letting it suffer past any purpose in the suffering. You have proven, this past turn of days, that you understand that too — in the cells, and at Corvane's border, in two decisions most men twice your age would have gotten wrong in one direction or the other." His hand rested a moment longer on the crossguard before he let it go entirely. "It is yours now. Carry it the way you carried the rest of it."

Lucian said nothing for a while, turning the blade slightly so the silver patterns caught the light and shifted, black to grey to black again, and found that the weight in his hands felt, after everything the last weeks had asked of him, less like an ending than like the first honest beginning he had been given in a very long time.

"I do not feel as though I have earned a reward," he said finally, still watching the way the light moved across the steel rather than his father's face. "I feel as though I have simply finished paying for something I could not have avoided owing."

"That is precisely why you have earned it, and precisely why I did not choose to give it to you sooner." Something in Vesper's voice carried the particular weight of a man handing down more than a blade. "A sword like this should never go to someone who wanted it for the pride of carrying it. It should go to someone who understands, all the way down, exactly what it costs to be trusted with an ending. You understand that now in a way you did not a season ago. That is the only qualification this house has ever truly cared about."

Lucian sheathed it at last, the black steel settling against his side with a weight that felt, already, less foreign than he had expected it to.
