# Chapter 67: Calm

The King had been gone six days when the first report came up the Ascend.

Aldric had stayed one night and most of a day, slept badly, eaten twice, and let Solara rest exactly as long as she could be persuaded to before he put himself back on her and went south again in the same grey hour he had arrived out of. A king absent from his own capital while a war opened was not a thing he intended to give anyone as a gift, he had said, and Vesper had not argued with him, and that had been the whole of the parting.

The rider was brought up to the solar still in his riding leathers, which Vesper allowed, and gave his report standing, which Vesper also allowed.

A Dravenhall host was on the march north. It had crossed out of Dravenhold's own highlands eight days ago and was moving at the pace of a force that intended to arrive intact rather than early. The number in it had been counted twice by two different sets of eyes before anyone had been willing to carry it this far, and the two counts had come back close enough to each other to be believed.

Vesper read the paper through, then handed it to Lucian without comment, which was its own kind of statement.

"Does it say what he means to do with it," Lucian said, when he had finished.

"It says he is coming north with everything he owns. Anything past that is a guess wearing a report's clothes." Vesper had not moved from the window. "There is one road into this duchy and it ends in my valley. Whether he means to take Firsthold, or means to take the mountain and needs the valley first to reach it, he arrives in precisely the same place either way. I will not know which until he is close enough that knowing has stopped being useful."

"Then we prepare for both."

"We prepare for both," Vesper agreed, "and we do it knowing that a house which prepares equally for two things has prepared insufficiently for either. That is not a flaw in the plan. That is the plan."

---

Dain came up the Ascend two days later and stayed for one evening, which was as long as he was willing to be away from the city.

He arrived with the valley's own arithmetic already written down and no particular patience for the parts of the discussion that were not it. Firsthold's walls were sound, which he pointed out meant only that they had never once been asked a serious question. The northern lesser houses were sending what they had; the first of them were already on the roads and the last of them would be later than anyone wanted. The granaries were better than they had any right to be, because Vesper had quietly kept them better for four years running without ever saying why.

"The Ascend," Valtor said, when it came to him. "How much of the Silver Guard do you want on the mountain."

"Most of it."

Dain looked up at that. Not an objection — Lucian had known his uncle long enough to read the difference — only a man making sure the thing had been said out loud where it could be examined.

"You will be thin in the valley."

"I will be thin in the valley," Vesper said. "If I am wrong about Firsthold, Firsthold suffers badly and I take it back from him in the spring, and I will carry what that costs for the rest of my life. If I am wrong about the mountain, there is no spring, and there is no house, and there is nobody left to carry anything." He let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "I would rather be wrong in the direction that leaves survivors."

Nobody argued. Lucian sat through the rest of it — the choke points, the two lower gates, which stretch of the Ascend could be brought down deliberately and which could not be brought down at all without cutting Firsthold off from its own keep — and noticed that at no point in the evening did any of the three most experienced military men in the North look upward past the Ascend's highest gate. Neither did he. The mountain had been unassailable for four hundred years by the only road that reached it, and there was no habit anywhere in the house of thinking past that road.

---

The harder question came at the end, when Dain had his cloak half on.

"There are sixty thousand people in my city," he said. "What am I doing with them."

"How many will go."

"Into the deep valleys? Some. The old mine settlements will take what they can feed and no more, and they are three days out with a cart, which means anyone who leaves is leaving now and leaving most of what they own." Dain's jaw worked once. "Up the Ascend I can take a few thousand and no more than that, and every one of them is a mouth on your summit and a body in the way of your own defense, and if he does come for the mountain I have simply moved them from one siege into a worse one."

"And the rest."

"The rest will not go at all." Dain said it flatly, without any of the bitterness the words could have carried. "They have been told for two hundred years that this house keeps the North safe. They believe it. They believe it the way they believe the sun comes up. So they will bar their doors and bank their fires and wait for us to be right, because it has never once in living memory occurred to any of them that we might not be."

Vesper was quiet a moment.

"Take who will come," he said. "Do not make them. And Dain — do not tell them it will be brief."

---

Lucian went down to the Shadow barracks the next morning and found Cazius already awake, already dressed, and already halfway through a list.

"Dawn stays on the mountain," he said. "Dain is sending several thousand people up the Ascend and nobody at that gate is going to know half their faces. Watch them. Quietly."

Cazius set the list aside and was gone before Lucian had reached the passage door, and that was the whole of it.

---

He flew in the afternoon, low over the valley, with the whole length of the approach unrolling beneath Ancalion in the flat white light of a northern autumn.

He had crossed this ground a thousand times without ever once looking at it properly — the river bends that would break a column's frontage, the two places where the valley narrowed enough to matter, the long open stretch in front of Firsthold's southern wall where there was nothing at all to hide behind and nothing to slow anyone down. It looked exactly as it had always looked. That was the part he could not get past. Somewhere south of the horizon a number that had been counted twice was walking north at the pace of men who meant to arrive intact, and the ground it was walking toward had not changed in any way and did not know.

Ancalion banked without being asked and held the turn, and through the bond came something Lucian had no clean word for — not fear, nothing so simple, and not eagerness either. Attention. The particular narrowing of a creature that had understood, somehow, that the shape of things had changed, and had not yet been told what it was expected to do about it.

*Not yet,* Lucian thought at him, and felt it received, and did not know whether he had meant it as reassurance or as a promise.

---

He wrote to Seren that night.

It took him four attempts and most of a candle, which was three attempts and most of a candle more than any letter he had sent her in the last two years, and the first three all failed the same way — he read them back and found the war in them, sitting underneath every second sentence like something at the bottom of clear water.

She would know soon enough. Everyone would know soon enough. And there was nothing whatsoever she could do with it from Aurelia except carry it, and he found he was not willing to hand her that when the handing served nothing but his own wish to have told her.

So he wrote her an ordinary letter.

He wrote about the North the way she had always asked him to and he had never once bothered to — that the light in autumn here came in flat and white and made the whole valley look like something drawn rather than grown, that the first snow was late this year and everyone had opinions about what that meant, that his grandmother had taken up an argument with the kitchens that both sides appeared to be enjoying. He wrote three full pages of things that did not matter. He read it back and found it was the best letter he had ever written her, and understood exactly why, and did not enjoy the understanding.

At the end of it he wrote:

*I have been a poor correspondent for a long time. I know that I have. I am not going to explain it, because I do not think there is an explanation that would improve it. I intend to be better. I would rather tell you that now than tell you later.*

He sealed it and sent it down before morning, on the last courier out of Blackspire that would beat the roads closing.

---

Vesper found him on the terrace two nights after that, and for a while neither of them said anything worth recording. Two of the northern houses had sent in their levies under strength and Vesper said so. The weather was going to break within the week and Lucian said so. Orlena had won her argument with the kitchens.

Then Vesper said, without any change in his voice at all, "How is Ancalion."

"Ready," Lucian said.

Vesper nodded slowly and went on looking out over the valley, and did not ask anything else, and Lucian understood — standing beside him in the cold with the whole of the North going dark below them — that his father had asked the only question he actually wanted answered, had received the answer, and had decided some while ago that the rest of it was no longer a thing he had standing to ask.

---

The column entered the valley eleven days later, in the middle of a clear grey morning, while Lucian was on the upper wall for no reason more urgent than that he had come to be on the upper wall every morning.

It came in at the far southern end, where the ground opened out, and for the first several minutes it was not an army at all but only a darkening — a slow change in the color of the land, the way a shadow crosses a field with no cloud above it to explain the shadow. Then it resolved, and kept resolving, and went on resolving for a very long time.

He watched it for perhaps a quarter of an hour without moving. Long enough to be certain. Long enough for the thing he had spent three weeks not deciding to finish not being a decision at all.

Then he turned from the wall and went down through the Black Halls to find his father, and the walk took exactly as long as it had always taken, and by the time he reached the solar door there was nothing left in him to work out.
