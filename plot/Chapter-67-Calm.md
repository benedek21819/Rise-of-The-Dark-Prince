# Chapter 67: Calm

The King had been gone six days when the first report came up the Ascend.

Aldric had stayed one night and most of a day, slept badly, eaten twice, and let Solara rest exactly as long as she could be persuaded to before he put himself back on her and went south again in the same grey hour he had arrived out of. A king absent from his own capital while a war opened was not a thing he intended to give anyone as a gift, he had said, and Vesper had not argued with him, and that had been the whole of the parting.

The rider was brought up to the solar still in his riding leathers, which Vesper allowed, and gave his report standing, which Vesper also allowed.

A Dravenhall host was on the march north. It had crossed out of Dravenhold's own highlands eight days ago and was moving at the pace of a force that intended to arrive intact rather than early. The number in it had been counted twice by two different sets of eyes before anyone had been willing to carry it this far, and the two counts had come back close enough to each other to be believed.

Vesper read the paper through, then handed it to Lucian without comment, which was its own kind of statement.

"He is bringing timber," Lucian said, when he had finished. "Engines, and the carts for them."

"He is."

"Then he is not raiding."

"No." Vesper had not moved from the window. "There is nothing north of the Iron Hills worth that march except this mountain. Firsthold is standing in the road, that is all."

Lucian set the paper down.

"Korrath, then."

"Korrath." Vesper turned from the window at last. "This keep was built against a road and nothing else. If he comes over the summit he does not need the Ascend at all."

"And you have an answer to that."

"Lirath. And the Weight."

Lucian looked up. He had seen it exactly once, in a closed room, standing still, as a demonstration that the word meant something.

"You have never used it where anyone could see."

"No." Vesper turned back to the window. "There is no version of losing this mountain in which the secret was worth keeping."

For a moment neither of them said anything.

"And Ancalion."

"Stays out of it."

He had known it was coming. It landed anyway.

"He is eighteen years old," Vesper said, before Lucian could put a word to it. "Korrath has killed dragons in a war, and by every account I have ever had of it, enjoyed the work. You are not in this plan. I would rather say that to you tonight than have you work it out in some corridor in three weeks and decide I had simply neglected to mention it."

"And if it goes badly."

"Then it goes badly with you alive," Vesper said. "That is the only arrangement of it I am prepared to accept."

---

Dain came up the Ascend two days later and stayed for one evening, which was as long as he was willing to be away from the city.

He arrived with the valley's own arithmetic already written down and no particular patience for the parts of the discussion that were not it. Firsthold's walls were sound, which he pointed out meant only that they had never once been asked a serious question. The northern lesser houses were sending what they had; the first were already on the roads and the last would be later than anyone wanted. The granaries were better than they had any right to be, because Vesper had quietly kept them better for four years running without ever saying why.

"The Ascend," Valtor said, when it came to him. "How much of the Silver Guard do you want on the mountain."

"Most of it."

Dain took that without argument, and asked the question underneath it instead.

"You expect Firsthold to hold."

"I expect Firsthold to hold." Vesper said it without any particular satisfaction. "He has marched the length of a continent to sit down in front of a walled city in the North, in front of winter, on ground that belongs to us, against the best-supplied army on this continent and every northern house that can still put men on a road. If that is genuinely the whole of his intention, he loses, and he can count as well as I can."

Dain was quiet for a moment.

"Then it is not the whole of his intention."

"No," Vesper said. "It is not."

Nobody in the room had anything to add to that.

"Hold the valley," Vesper said at last. "Do not chase him. Do not go out to meet him, and do not let him pull you into open ground however cheap he makes it look. Whatever he actually means to do, it is not going to happen where he is standing, and I would rather you were not out in front of the walls when it does."

Ancalion's name came up once near the end. Vesper gave the same answer he had given in the solar, in front of both of them, and nobody asked him for it a second time.

Later, lying awake with the fire down to almost nothing, Lucian ran his father's reasoning three times looking for the place it failed, and it came out the same way every time. That was the part he could not put back down. Not that Vesper was wrong — only that all of it now rested on one man and one dragon, and the only thing in the North that could have stood behind them had been set aside deliberately, and out of love.

---

The harder question came at the end, when Dain had his cloak half on.

"There are sixty thousand people in my city," he said. "What am I doing with them."

"How many will go."

"Into the deep valleys? Some. The old mine settlements will take what they can feed and no more, and they are three days out with a cart, which means anyone who leaves is leaving now and leaving most of what they own." Dain's jaw worked once. "Up the Ascend I can take a few thousand and no more than that, and every one of them is a mouth on your summit and a body in the way of your own defense."

"And the rest."

"The rest will not go at all." Dain said it flatly, without any of the bitterness the words could have carried. "They have been told for two hundred years that this house keeps the North safe. They believe it the way they believe the sun comes up. So they will bar their doors and bank their fires and wait for us to be right, because it has never once in living memory occurred to any of them that we might not be."

Vesper was quiet a moment.

"Take who will come," he said. "Do not make them. And Dain — do not tell them it will be brief."

---

Lucian went down to the Shadow barracks the next morning and found Cazius already awake, already dressed, and already halfway through a list.

"Dawn stays on the mountain," he said. "Dain is sending several thousand people up the Ascend and nobody at that gate is going to know half their faces. Watch them. Quietly."

Cazius set the list aside and was gone before Lucian had reached the passage door, and that was the whole of it.

---

He flew in the afternoon, low over the valley, with the whole length of the approach unrolling beneath Ancalion in the flat white light of a northern autumn.

He had crossed this ground a thousand times without ever once looking at it properly — the river bends that would break a column's frontage, the two places where the valley narrowed enough to matter, the long open stretch in front of Firsthold's southern wall where there was nothing to hide behind and nothing to slow anyone down. It looked exactly as it had always looked. That was the part he could not get past. Somewhere south of the horizon a number that had been counted twice was walking north at the pace of men who meant to arrive intact, and the ground it was walking toward had not changed in any way and did not know.

Ancalion banked without being asked and held the turn, and through the bond came something Lucian had no clean word for — not fear, nothing so simple, and not eagerness either. Attention. The particular narrowing of a creature that had understood that the shape of things had changed, and had not yet been told what it was expected to do about it.

*Not yet,* Lucian thought at him, and felt it received, and did not know whether he had meant it as reassurance or as a promise.

---

He wrote to Seren that night.

It took him four attempts and most of a candle, which was three attempts and most of a candle more than any letter he had sent her in the last two years, and the first three all failed the same way — he read them back and found the war in them, sitting underneath every second sentence like something at the bottom of clear water.

She would know soon enough, and there was nothing she could do with it from Aurelia except carry it.

So he wrote her an ordinary letter.

He wrote about the North the way she had always asked him to and he had never once bothered to — that the light in autumn here came in flat and white and made the whole valley look like something drawn rather than grown, that the first snow was late this year and everyone had opinions about what that meant, that his grandmother had taken up an argument with the kitchens that both sides appeared to be enjoying. He wrote three full pages of things that did not matter. He read it back and found it was the best letter he had ever written her, and understood exactly why, and did not enjoy the understanding.

At the end of it he wrote:

*I have been a poor correspondent for a long time. I know that I have. I am not going to explain it, because I do not think there is an explanation that would improve it. I intend to be better. I would rather tell you that now than tell you later.*

He sealed it and sent it down before morning, on the last courier out of Blackspire that would beat the roads closing.

---

Vesper found him on the terrace two nights after that, and for a while neither of them said anything worth recording. Two of the northern houses had sent their levies in under strength and Vesper said so. The weather was going to break within the week and Lucian said so. Orlena had won her argument with the kitchens.

Then Vesper said, without any change in his voice at all, "How is Ancalion."

"Ready," Lucian said.

Vesper nodded slowly, went on looking out over the valley, and asked nothing else.

---

The column entered the valley eleven days later, in the middle of a clear grey morning, while Lucian was on the upper wall for no reason more urgent than that he had come to be on the upper wall every morning.

It came in at the far southern end, where the ground opened out, and for the first several minutes it was not an army at all but only a darkening — a slow change in the color of the land, the way a shadow crosses a field with no cloud above it to explain the shadow. Then it resolved, and kept resolving, and went on resolving for a very long time.

He watched it for perhaps a quarter of an hour without moving. Long enough to be certain.

Then he turned from the wall and went down through the Black Halls to find his father, and the walk took exactly as long as it had always taken, and by the time he reached the solar door there was nothing left in him to work out.
