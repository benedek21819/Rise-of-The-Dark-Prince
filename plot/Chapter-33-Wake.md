# Chapter 33: Wake

The formal farewell was held in the same private study where the visit had effectively begun, which Lucian understood, by now, as its own kind of message — the King closing the account in the same room he had opened it, rather than in the throne room where an audience might read more into the gesture than either house wished given.

"You leave with more of the kingdom's regard than you arrived with," Aldric said, rising to clasp Vesper's forearm the way he had on the first morning. "I do not say that lightly, and I do not say it only for the sake of the alliance. Aurelia does not forget a black dragon crossing its sky for the first time in living memory, whatever else it forgets by the time the next season's gossip arrives to replace it."

"We are grateful for the welcome," Vesper said. "And for the candor, three days ago, that a lesser king might not have offered a guest he could not yet fully trust."

"I offered it because I judged you worth the risk of offering it." Aldric's gaze moved briefly to Lucian, something steadier in it now than the tired formality of their first meeting. "What we discussed remains where we left it. I will not move loudly, and I will not move quickly, but I will move. You have my word on that, for whatever a king's word is worth against a house that has already given more to mine than it has ever asked returned."

"It is worth a great deal, Your Majesty," Lucian said. "I have learned to recognize the difference between words spent and words merely offered."

Something in Aldric's expression eased at that, tired but genuine, and beside him Queen Isolde inclined her head with the same composed grace she had carried through every hour of the visit Lucian had witnessed her in — warm enough on its surface to satisfy any watching eye, and, beneath it, entirely unreadable in a way Lucian had stopped expecting to solve during a single visit. He thought, briefly, of a courtyard crossed at a careful distance, a senior scholar's low voice, a door that had closed the way doors close on business no one intended a passing stranger to understand. He said nothing of it. There was, he had learned from his father across a year that had cost him more than he liked to count, a considerable difference between noticing a thing and being ready to act on it.

"Lord Caldarion." Isolde's voice was even, pleasant, giving nothing away that she had not already decided to give. "I hope Aurelia has treated you and your brothers kindly. Seren speaks of little else, when she thinks no one of consequence is listening."

"She has been generous with her time," Lucian said. "More generous than I have always deserved."

"That has always been her particular gift." Something flickered behind the Queen's composed warmth, there and gone before it could be named — not hostility, not quite, but a watchfulness that matched, in its own careful way, the watchfulness Lucian had learned to carry himself. "I hope your house remembers it kindly."

"We will."

The audience closed without further ceremony, and the household's actual leave-taking, looser and warmer than the study had allowed, happened in the courtyard below where the carriages already stood loaded and the guard had begun the last of its preparations. Corvin found Lucian first, quiet as ever, offering nothing more than a level nod and a handshake that held a fraction longer than courtesy required — the kind of gesture, Lucian understood, that meant more from a boy who spent nothing on gestures he didn't intend. Dorian was harder to read, correct and civil in front of the gathered household, but he found a moment near the gate, low enough that only Lucian caught it, to say that he would send word if anything worth sending ever crossed his desk regarding Roderic — a small thing, freely offered, that cost Dorian something Lucian suspected he did not often spend.

Seren found him last, near the carriages, where the noise of departure gave the two of them the closest thing to privacy the morning had allowed.

"You are leaving before I have finished being annoyed with you for something," she said, which he had learned by now was simply how she said most things that mattered to her.

"What have I done this time?"

"Nothing yet. I am keeping the annoyance in reserve, for whatever you eventually do." She looked up at him, the pale blue eyes steady in the way they had always been steady, since the first courtyard two years before. "I meant what I said at the river. I am not in a hurry. But I intend to still be here, wanting the same things, the next time you come south — and I would rather you came expecting that than surprised by it."

"I will come expecting it."

"Good." She stepped back, satisfied, as the household began its final gathering toward the carriages, and something in her expression settled into the particular certainty he had come, across the length of the visit, to recognize as entirely her own. "Fly safely, Lucian. The sky does not belong to you simply because you have grown used to it."

He held that thought with him through the climb back to the field beyond the palace wall, where Ancalion waited exactly as he had waited three days before — restless in the confines of the walled roost, visibly easier the moment the open sky came back into view above him. The launch took the ground away in the same controlled surge it always did, and this time Lucian did not linger over the city rising and falling away beneath him, golden and layered and entirely unlike the mountain that had built him. He held the altitude instead and found the dark, orderly thread of carriages already moving north along the river road, his father's household setting out for the long way home, and let Ancalion bank low enough, once, that anyone watching from the lead carriage might have marked a dragon's shadow pass deliberately over the black and silver banners below.

The wind carried the whole of the visit with him as the city fell away — a queen who gave nothing away and had visited a scholars' hall she had no obvious reason to visit; a prince who wore a crown he had not chosen and, however warily, no longer counted himself an enemy; a magister who knew more than he said and seemed, faintly, aware that Lucian had noticed; two brothers who had come apart, cleanly, into two separate people for the first time in his memory; a girl by a river who intended to still be there, patient as the current itself, the next time he came looking for her.

Blackspire rose out of the northern haze hours later exactly as it always had, cold and ordered and unmistakably home, and Lucian understood, banking Ancalion into the final long glide toward the roosts, that he was returning with considerably more than he had carried south — not answers, not yet, but the particular unsettled weight of questions that had finally learned each other's shape well enough to be carried properly.
