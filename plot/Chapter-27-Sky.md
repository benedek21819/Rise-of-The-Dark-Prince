# Chapter 27: Sky

Vesper gave the permission the same way he gave most things that mattered — without ceremony, in a sentence that closed the matter before Lucian had fully understood it had been opened.

“You will fly to Aurelia. The rest of us will go by the road.”

They were in the lower solar, the morning after the household had finished its preparations for the journey south, and for a moment Lucian only looked at his father, waiting for the qualification that had followed every mention of Ancalion's wings since the reprimand on the high terrace years before. It did not come.

“You restricted the city from me a season ago,” Lucian said, careful rather than accusing. “Now you give me the sky.”

“They are not the same permission, and I have not forgotten why I gave the first one back.” Vesper's gaze was level, unreadable in its usual way, though something in it held still a moment longer than strict necessity required. “Firsthold's danger was real and unseen. This is neither. Every eye between here and Aurelia will know exactly what they are looking at when they see a black dragon in open sky, and that is the entire purpose of allowing it. Caution restricted you once because the threat could not be seen coming. It will not restrict you now, because I intend for this to be seen by everyone.”

“A show of strength.”

“A statement,” Vesper said. “House Caldarion does not send its heir to Aurelia in a carriage like a supplicant. It sends him through the air, on a dragon grown too large to be politely overlooked, and lets every duchy between here and the capital draw its own conclusions.” A pause. “You have earned the flight on its own terms as well. I would not risk you in open sky merely to make a point. Dain tells me your control is sufficient. I have chosen to believe him.”

It was, Lucian understood, as close to an apology for the restriction in Firsthold as his father was likely to offer, folded inside a decision that owed nothing to apology at all.

The roosts were colder than the halls below, the wind sharper for the open sky beyond the platform's edge, and Ancalion was already waiting when Lucian climbed the last stretch of stone — black scales without sheen, deep purple eyes fixed on him with the same immediate, wordless recognition that had never once dimmed in fourteen years. He was no longer the lean, restless shape Lucian remembered from the high platform of his childhood. The wingspan alone, folded, rose well above Lucian's own head now, and the chest and shoulders beneath the black scales had thickened into something that no longer looked merely fast. He looked, for the first time in Lucian's memory, dangerous in the way Lirath was dangerous — a settled, considered danger rather than a coiled one.

Lucian set his palm against the warm scale at the base of the long neck, and the bond opened the way it always opened, immediate and complete, though today it carried an edge of something sharper than the ordinary quiet recognition — anticipation, restless and barely leashed, answering something in Lucian that he had not fully admitted to himself until he felt it mirrored back.

“You know where we're going,” he said, quietly, more to himself than to the dragon. “Further than the mountain's shoulder, this time.”

Ancalion's low answering sound vibrated through the stone underfoot.

There was no formal saddle rig beyond the simple harness Cole had fitted that morning, and no ceremony to the mounting beyond Lucian finding the familiar seat just behind the base of the neck, one hand set against warm scale, the whole of his weight settling into a place that felt less like sitting atop something and more like completing it. Dain stood at the platform's edge, arms folded, watching with the same flat assessment he brought to every hour of training — but something had eased in the set of his shoulders that had not been there when Lucian first climbed the stair.

“Hold the line south along the river valleys,” Dain said. “He knows the shape of the journey. Trust him with it, and correct nothing he has not already gotten wrong.”

“Yes, Instructor.”

“Uncle, today.” The faintest suggestion of something almost warm crossed Dain's face. “Go.”

Ancalion did not wait for a second instruction.

The launch took the ground away from beneath them in a single controlled surge, wings driving hard against the thin cold air until the platform, the mountain, the whole familiar architecture of Blackspire fell into a shrinking pattern of dark stone against white. Lucian's stomach dropped with the climb the way it always had on the old low glides of his childhood, but this time the drop did not resolve into the mountain's shoulder curving back beneath them. It kept opening. The peaks fell away in long white ridges, then in isolated white teeth scattered across a darkening land, then in nothing at all — only sky, wide and pale and entirely unbroken, and the whole of the Northern Duchy laid out below them like a map made real.

He had never been this high. He understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the Veil, that he had never truly understood the size of his own home until it became small enough to hold in a single glance.

They leveled into a long, driving glide south, and the cold up here was a different creature than the cold of the training halls — cleaner, thinner, carrying none of the stone's stubborn memory, only the pure fact of altitude. Lucian kept low against Ancalion's neck, more from instinct than instruction, and let the wind take the water from his eyes without trying to blink it away. Below, the mountains gave way by degrees to foothills, the foothills to the first dark green smudges of forest, the forest to fields laid out in careful measured strips he had only ever seen described in maps.

Midway through the first hour, he found the procession.

It was small from this height, a dark, orderly thread of carriages and outriders moving along the southern road, banners too distant to read but unmistakable all the same — the black and silver, small as a single struck note against the pale road, carrying his mother and his brothers toward the same destination by the slower, older way. Lucian held the altitude and let Ancalion bank once, wide and unhurried, low enough that a keen eye below might have marked the shape of a dragon passing without alarm, high enough that nothing on the ground could mistake it for a threat. He did not know if Elara looked up. He liked to think she did.

Ancalion's attention followed his own down toward the moving thread of black and silver, and something in the bond eased further, some last thin thread of restlessness settling now that the rest of what he guarded was accounted for and visible.

They flew on.

The land changed again as the hours passed — the strict, ordered fields of the mid-kingdom duchies, a wide grey river catching the afternoon light in a long unbroken ribbon, the first suggestion of a city larger than any Lucian had ever seen crouched pale and golden at the place where the river bent. He felt Ancalion's wingbeats shift, adjusting for a headwind Lucian could not yet feel himself, and understood that they were closer to the end of the flight than he had let himself believe.

Aurelia rose out of the haze by degrees, the way Blackspire never had to, because Blackspire had never needed to announce itself twice. Here the city climbed in visible layers from the riverfront upward — dark clustered roofs first, then wider avenues, then pale stone rising toward a high district crowned in white and gold that caught the lowering sun and held it, unwilling to let it go. It was nothing like home. It did not try to be. Where Blackspire was a closed fist raised against the cold, Aurelia was an open hand, spread wide and confident across the center of the continent it ruled, certain of its own light in a way the North had never once had the luxury of being certain of anything.

Lucian felt something in his chest that he did not immediately have the word for, and only recognized it, as Ancalion began his long final descent toward the open grounds beyond the river's noble quarter, as something adjacent to wonder — an unfamiliar shape for a boy raised to distrust anything that arrived without cost.

Below, faces were already turning upward. He could see them even from this height, small pale ovals tilting back along the riverbank, along the bridges, along the high terraces of the palace itself — a city that had never in living memory seen a Caldarion dragon in its own sky, watching one arrive now with a stillness that carried, even at this distance, the particular quality of a held breath.

Ancalion came down in a long, controlled sweep onto the open field beyond the palace's outer wall, wings driving hard against the ground in the last moment to break the speed of the landing, and the impact traveled up through Lucian's whole body as familiar and certain as anything Blackspire had ever taught him. The wind of the passage rolled outward across the grass in a visible wave, flattening it, and for a long moment neither dragon nor rider moved, the flight's whole distance still singing faintly in Lucian's blood.

He slid down from the harness onto solid ground that felt, after so many hours of sky, subtly wrong beneath his boots — too still, too fixed, as though the earth itself had forgotten how to move the way the wind had moved beneath them for the length of an afternoon.

Around the field's edge, at a respectful and clearly startled distance, a scattering of palace guards and gathering onlookers stood in a silence that had not yet decided whether to become alarm or awe. Lucian set his hand once more against Ancalion's neck, grounding himself in the bond the way he always did, and looked toward the pale, golden sprawl of the city rising beyond the wall — bright, layered, utterly unlike anything he had been built by.

He had arrived before his family. He had arrived, he understood, exactly the way his father had intended him to.

Behind him, Ancalion folded his wings and settled, watchful and enormous against the darkening grass, and did not look away from the gathered crowd until Lucian gave him leave to.
