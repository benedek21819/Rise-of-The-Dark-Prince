# Chapter 30: Crown

The invitation came the third morning, delivered by a page rather than spoken in person, which told Lucian something about its sender before he had even broken the seal. *The Crown Prince requests the pleasure of Lord Lucian Caldarion's company in the east training court, at the hour before midday.* Formal enough to refuse only at a cost. Informal enough that refusing would look, to anyone who heard of it, like fear rather than caution.

He went.

The east training court was smaller than the one at Blackspire, floored in pale sand rather than packed earth, ringed by a colonnade that let the noble quarter's idle watch the sparring below without ever being asked whether they wished to. Dorian was already there when Lucian arrived, sleeves rolled, a practice blade loose in one hand, and three of his own household guard standing at a distance calculated to look like privacy while remaining close enough to witness whatever happened.

"Lord Caldarion." Dorian's smile was easy, practiced, the kind that had clearly worked on rooms less inclined to look past it. "I thought we might spar. Brother to brother, in a manner of speaking, given what the betrothal makes of us eventually."

"I did not know we were brothers yet."

"Not yet." Something flickered behind the smile, there and gone before it could be called an expression in its own right. "Soon enough, if the arrangement holds the course everyone insists it will. I thought it worth knowing the man my sister has attached herself to, before the paperwork makes the knowing obligatory rather than chosen."

It was, Lucian recognized, almost exactly the shape of the steward's visit two years before — a thing offered in the shape of warmth that carried, underneath, a colder and more particular purpose. He took the practice blade a guard offered him and tested its balance, unhurried, giving himself the moment his father's training had taught him to take before answering anything that mattered.

"Then let us know each other," Lucian said.

They came together in the center of the sand without further ceremony, and within the first exchange Lucian understood two things at once: that Dorian's talent was real, trained by tutors who had not wasted their time on him, and that it had never once been tested against anything that did not already expect to lose to a prince. He gave ground where giving ground cost him nothing, let Dorian press an advantage that existed only because Lucian had allowed the opening, and watched, with the same flat attention he brought to reading a room, how Dorian's confidence built on exactly the foundation Lucian had built it on.

"You are better than the rumors suggested," Dorian said, breathing harder than the exchange should have cost him, circling. "I confess I expected more of the cold reputation and less of the actual craft behind it."

"The reputation is not meant to be accurate. It is meant to be useful."

"Spoken like a man who has been taught the difference." Dorian came in again, faster, and this time Lucian met the strike squarely rather than yielding to it, feeling the jolt travel up through wrist and forearm in a way that told him precisely how much force Dorian had actually committed. Not everything. Enough to test, not enough to risk looking foolish if the test failed. "Tell me, Lucian — may I call you that, given the circumstances? — does your father teach you that everything is a weapon, or only the obvious ones?"

"He teaches that everything is a cost. Whether it becomes a weapon depends on who is paying."

Dorian's blade paused a half-beat longer than the exchange required, and something behind his eyes sharpened into the actual purpose of the morning, the pleasantry finally thinning enough to show what it had been built around.

"Firsthold, then," he said. "Since we are speaking of costs."

"You have already heard what there is to hear of it."

"I have heard what my father chose to tell me, which is considerably less than what I suspect passed between him and your father in that study three days ago." Dorian circled again, blade low, and the smile had gone entirely now, replaced by something more honest and considerably less pleasant. "I am not a fool, whatever House Caldarion prefers to believe of me. I know my uncle's temper. I know the shape of a message meant to look like an accident. And I know that my father would rather manage a suspicion quietly than risk the alliance that keeps this kingdom from tearing itself apart the way it did the last time a Kaelarion king proved weak enough to be tested."

"Then you understand the situation as well as I do."

"I understand it better." Dorian's voice had lost the last of its performance, and for the length of a breath Lucian saw, underneath the ambition and the practiced charm, something rawer and more familiar than he had expected to find in a prince who resented him. "Do you know what it is to grow up the heir to a throne that everyone agrees is only still standing because another house's dragons keep it upright? I did not choose this arrangement between our families. I inherited it, the same as you inherited yours, and I have spent every year since old enough to understand it wondering what will be left of my own authority once I actually wear the crown my father is so tired of carrying."

"You will wear a crown," Lucian said, and something in the flatness of it landed harder than he had intended. "My house has never worn one at all."

Dorian's blade stilled entirely.

"Explain that."

"There is an old saying in my family. *No crown without weight.*" Lucian lowered his own blade, unhurried, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he wanted Dorian to actually hear this rather than simply be maneuvered past it. "No Caldarion has worn one, not since the first, not through everything that came after him. Some in my house read it as warning — that no one should wear a crown who has not already proven he can carry what it costs. Others read it as its opposite — that a man who already carries the true weight has no need of the symbol at all." He held Dorian's eyes. "You have the symbol waiting for you already, given by birth rather than earned by anything you have yet done. I understand why that might feel like an advantage. I am not certain it is one."

For a long moment Dorian said nothing, the practice blade forgotten at his side, and something moved behind his expression that Lucian could not fully read — not gratitude, nothing so simple as that, but something closer to a man being told a truth he had suspected privately and never once heard said aloud by someone with no obvious reason to comfort him.

Then it closed over, the way a wound closes over when the person carrying it decides, deliberately, that showing it further would cost more than it was worth.

"That is a very convenient philosophy," Dorian said, "for a house that would lose nothing real by never wearing one. Easy to call the crown unnecessary when you already hold every advantage a crown is meant to provide, without ever having to answer to the people who put it on you." He raised his blade again, and the smile came back, thinner now, more effort in it than before. "I did not ask you here to be lectured on my own family's philosophy, Lord Caldarion. I asked because I wished to see, for myself, whether the boy attached to my sister was worth the trouble everyone insists he is."

"And your judgment?"

"Undecided." Dorian struck, faster and with more real force than anything before it, and Lucian met it, turned it, let the exchange run its course without pressing the advantage he could have taken twice over. "I will tell you this much freely, since you were at least honest with me a moment ago, whatever your reasons. I do not trust your house, Lucian. I do not think I ever will, not fully, regardless of what Seren feels for you or what my father owes yours. But I find I trust you slightly more this morning than I did an hour ago, which is either your doing or a very well-executed trick, and I have not yet decided which."

"Time will answer that better than I can."

"Yes." Dorian stepped back, lowering his blade, and something in his bearing had shifted — not warmth, not yet, but a wariness that had cooled from active hostility into something more like careful assessment. "It generally does, in this family. We are all extremely patient about the things that matter and extremely impatient about everything else." He handed his practice blade to the nearest guard, the performance of the morning fully spent now, and looked at Lucian with an expression that held, for the first time since the invitation had arrived, nothing rehearsed at all. "Firsthold will not go unanswered. Whatever my father decides to do about it, quietly or otherwise — I want you to know that I would not have stopped him, whatever else you may think of me. Roderic is my uncle. He is not, and has never been, my friend."

He left the training court without waiting for a reply, his guards falling into step behind him, and Lucian stood alone in the pale sand for a long moment afterward, turning the morning over the way he turned over everything Dain had never directly taught him.

He had come expecting a rival. He left with something less simple than that — not an ally, not yet, and perhaps not ever entirely, but a man who wore a crown he had not chosen and resented it in ways Lucian, house of the crownless, was only beginning to understand.
