# Chapter 29: Water

The reception had been exactly as tedious as Lucian's father had promised it would not be tedious, in the particular way adults promised children things they both understood to be false the moment they were said. Isolde had smiled at Vesper across the width of a room that made the smile easy to give and harder to trust. Dorian had asked him three careful, pointed questions about Ancalion's flight speed that were not, Lucian understood by the second one, actually questions about the dragon. Corvin had said almost nothing at all and watched everything, which Lucian found he respected more than anything Dorian had managed. It had gone on for three hours, and Lucian had spent every one of them standing correctly, speaking little, and thinking of the river he had seen from the window that morning, wide and unhurried below the noble quarter, entirely unlike anything Blackspire had ever offered him.

He found it the next morning instead, before the household had fully woken, on the strength of a route one of the residence guards had described to him without being asked twice. The gardens ran down from the noble quarter in wide terraced steps to a stretch of bank where the current slowed enough that the water lay almost still, catching the early light in long unbroken sheets. It was nothing like the thermal pools at Firsthold, all stone and enclosed heat. It moved. It kept moving, patient and certain, the way nothing at Blackspire ever needed to be patient about anything.

She was already there when he arrived, which he had not expected and which explained, in hindsight, why the guard had known the route so readily.

"You found it too," Seren said, not turning from the water at first, as though she had heard his step and already known whose it was. She was two years older than the last time he had seen her properly, in the courtyard at Blackspire's gate — taller, though not by much, the girlishness of nine settling into something steadier without losing the openness that had always been the whole of her. "I come here every morning I am home. Corvin thinks I do it to avoid the receptions. He is not entirely wrong."

"You avoided last night's."

"I did not avoid it. I endured it and then required somewhere quiet afterward, which is not the same thing, though I understand why you might confuse the two." She turned then, and the pale blue eyes that had not looked away first in a courtyard two years ago did not look away first now either. "You stood very still all evening. I watched you do it. I thought, once, that you might actually say something to Dorian that was not entirely careful, and then you did not, and I was almost disappointed."

"He was fishing for information about Ancalion's speed in open combat. I did not intend to hand it to him at a reception."

"That is exactly the kind of thing you would notice and I would not." She said it without envy, simple observation rather than comparison, and sat back down on the low stone edge of the bank, leaving room beside her that he took without deciding to. "I only noticed that his questions made you tired in a way the rest of the evening did not."

He considered denying it, out of habit more than conviction, and found the habit did not hold the way it once had. "They did."

"Good. I like knowing when I am right about you. It happens rarely enough that I keep count." She trailed her fingers into the water, watching the current pull small rings out from where they touched, and for a while neither of them said anything further, the river filling the silence the way silence at Blackspire never quite managed to fill itself. "I heard about Firsthold," she said eventually, quieter than anything else she had said so far. "My father told me some of it. Not all of it, I think, but some."

"It is finished."

"That is not the same as it not having happened." She looked at him properly then, and whatever warmth usually sat easily on her face had gone briefly serious, older than her eleven years. "I am glad you were not hurt. I know that is an obvious thing to say. I wanted to say it anyway, because I do not think anyone else has said it to you plainly, the way I mean it, without also wanting something from you at the same time."

Lucian looked at the water rather than at her, because it was easier to hold what she had said if he was not also holding her expression while he held it. "You do not want something from me?"

"I want a great many things from you." She said it lightly, but something underneath the lightness was entirely serious. "I am not pretending otherwise. But I did not say that just now because I wanted anything back for it. I said it because it was true, and because I think you do not hear enough things that are only true and nothing else."

He did not have a ready answer for that, the way he had not had one for Lyra in the gallery after Firsthold, and found that the two silences, though different in every other way, shared the same shape — something offered without a price attached, that he was still learning how to simply receive.

"You have not asked me anything I do not need to know yet," he said finally, which was as close as he came to acknowledging any of it directly.

"I am saving it." Something of her old brightness returned, quick and unforced. "I learned that from you, actually. You never say the interesting thing first either."

"I do not know that I have an interesting thing to say."

"You always do. You simply decide, beforehand, that no one has earned it yet." She rose, brushing the damp from her skirts, and looked down at him with an expression that held equal parts patience and certainty, as though she were reading a distance she alone could measure. "I intend to earn it eventually. I am in no hurry. The water does not hurry either, and it still gets exactly where it means to go."

She left him there on the bank without waiting for a reply, the way she had once left him in the gallery two years before, and this time he did not watch her go with irritation trailing after her. He watched the current instead, wide and unhurried and entirely certain of its own direction, and understood, distantly, that she had just told him something true about herself that he was not yet equipped to fully hear.

Behind him, further up the terraced steps, a smaller shape detached itself from the shadow of a garden wall and did not bother pretending it had not been watching. Corvin crossed the last stretch of grass unhurried, hands loose at his sides, and settled onto the stone beside Lucian without asking permission the way Dorian would have.

"She talks to the water like it listens," Corvin said, by way of greeting. "She has done that since we were small. I used to think it was strange. I have stopped thinking that."

"Why."

"Because she is usually right about the things she says to it first, before she says them to anyone else." Corvin looked out at the current, unbothered, the same watchful quiet he had carried through the whole of last night's reception. "I do not know what that means. I only know it is true often enough that I have stopped being surprised by it."

Lucian said nothing to that, but he filed it away the way he filed most things Dain had never directly taught him — without understanding yet what it was for, only that it belonged somewhere in the shape of everything he was still learning to see.
