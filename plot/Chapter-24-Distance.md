# Chapter 24: Distance

The weeks after the visit south folded into one another without marking themselves clearly apart.

Lucian trained. That was the whole of it, reduced to its simplest shape — mornings in the lower chamber with Dain, the Foundation drilled past the point of thought, Weight pursued in the strange, wordless way Dain insisted it had to be pursued, through repetition rather than explanation. Afternoons he often returned alone, unassigned, the way he had before the choice was ever spoken aloud in that hall. No one told him to. He simply found himself there, more often than not, when the rest of the keep had moved on to other hours.

He did not think of it as excessive. It did not occur to him to think of it in those terms at all. The forms held clean now through longer stretches than they once had — Dain had said as much, in the sparse way he said anything resembling approval — and each session that ended without a fault felt like the natural continuation of the one before it, not an accumulation of anything that might eventually cost him.

Dain noticed before Lucian did, though he said little of it directly.

“Your guard drops on the fifth repetition now,” he said, one grey afternoon indistinguishable from the others. “It did not, three weeks ago.”

“I will correct it.”

“That is not the correction I am asking for.” Dain studied him a moment longer, the same unhurried, assessing look he gave a fault in a stance. “A blade held too long past its edge does not sharpen further. It only wears.”

Lucian did not fully understand the remark, and did not ask him to clarify it. He reset his stance instead, and the guard held on the fifth repetition, and he took that as the matter answered.

Lyra found him in the lower corridor two days later, in the thin grey hour after a session that had run longer than the ones before it. She did not announce herself. She simply fell into step beside him the way she had done since he was small enough that she'd needed to shorten her stride to match his, and studied his face with the particular attention she gave nothing else in the keep.

“You are pale,” she said.

“I trained.”

“You always train. This is different.” She reached out, the way she had a thousand times before, and this time did not straighten his collar or smooth a fold from his cloak — only rested the back of her fingers briefly against his cheek, cool against skin that felt, to him, entirely ordinary. Her mouth tightened faintly at whatever she found there.

“I am not tired,” Lucian said, because it was true, as far as he could tell. The words came out steady. His legs, when he considered them, held his weight without complaint. Nothing in him registered as depleted. He did not know what she thought she had found.

“No,” Lyra agreed, in the tone of someone humoring a claim she had already decided was wrong. “You wouldn't know if you were. That is rather the problem.”

He had no answer for that. It was not the kind of statement that admitted one.

“Come with me,” she said, and did not make it a request in any way that left room for the alternative.

She led him down through colder, older passages than the ones he usually walked — past the training levels, past even the forges, into a narrower stretch of the mountain's lower reaches where the air began, unexpectedly, to soften. The stone changed character by degrees, damp where it had been dry, warmer where it should by every rule of Blackspire have been colder still. A faint mineral scent reached him before the passage opened into a low, uneven chamber lit by a scatter of unobtrusive lamps, where pale steam rose in slow curls from a wide pool set into the rock.

“The springs,” Lyra said, as though the name alone should have meant something to him. “You've never been.”

“No.”

“I know.” She said it without reproach, only a kind of tired certainty, as if his never having come here were simply one more fact in a long accounting she had kept without telling him she was keeping it. “Mother brought me, when I was younger than you are now. I have brought Selene twice, though she mostly wanted to see whether she could make the water boil by shouting at it.”

Lucian looked at the pool, the slow drift of steam catching the lamplight, and did not immediately understand what he was meant to do with a room that asked nothing of him.

“Sit,” Lyra said. “That is the whole of the instruction. You are permitted to do only that, for as long as you like, and no one will come looking for you or correcting your stance while you do it.”

He sat at the pool's edge, and after a moment, because the water's heat reached him even there and some part of his body he had not consulted decided on its own that this was preferable to standing, he lowered himself into it. The warmth closed around him gradually, nothing like the shock of cold he was built to withstand without flinching — this asked no resistance of him at all, only permission, and his shoulders dropped by a degree he had not realized they'd been holding.

Lyra sat at the edge nearby, not entering, her boots off and her feet in the shallow warm water at the pool's lip, watching him with the same attention she had given his face in the corridor.

“Better,” she said, after a while, not quite a question.

“I do not know what better means, in this context.”

“No,” she said again, gently this time. “I don't suppose you would.”

They sat without speaking for a stretch he did not measure. The lamps threw soft, unsteady light against the wet stone. Somewhere above them, the ordinary cold weight of the keep continued on without him, the training halls and the corridors and the whole exacting architecture of what he was becoming, and for the length of this one warm hour none of it required anything of him at all.

“You push yourself the way Father pushes the house,” Lyra said eventually, quiet, not looking at him directly. “As if stopping were the same as failing.”

“It is not the same.”

“I know that. I am not certain you do, not yet.” She drew her feet from the water and folded her arms loosely over her knees. “I only want you to still be someone, underneath all of it, when it is finished being built. Not only the thing it built.”

Lucian did not have an answer for that either. He let the water hold him instead, warm and undemanding, and found that the absence of an answer did not feel, for once, like a failure to provide one.

“I will remember this place exists,” he said finally, which was not quite agreement, and was, he understood even as he said it, the largest concession available to him.

Lyra's mouth curved, small and real. “That will do, for now.”

She did not press him further. She sat with her feet in the warm water and let the silence be exactly as much as either of them could comfortably hold, and Lucian, submerged to the shoulders in heat he had not known his own house contained, let the ache he had not recognized as an ache slowly, imperceptibly begin to loosen.
