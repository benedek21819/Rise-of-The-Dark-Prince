# Chapter 46: Rot

"I want to return to something you said in the cells below this keep," Vesper said, "the night we decided what became of the nine men who tried to take you."

Lucian looked up from the report he had been reading without truly reading it, some minor matter of grain stores that had suddenly stopped holding his attention entirely. "I said a great many things that night."

"You said they knew exactly whose life they were reaching for. That they had been given reason, however borrowed, to want the taking of it." Vesper stood at the solar's window, the same window he so often stood at when he intended to say something he had already turned over privately many times before allowing it into the room. "I have not been able to set the sentence down since. Because knowing whose life to reach for is not the same thing as knowing where and when that life would be standing on an otherwise unremarkable stretch of road, cut off from the rest of its escort, at the one hour in three days when it would be least protected."

"The parley's route was decided the morning we rode for Corvane's hall. Valtor chose it."

"Valtor chose it that morning, in this keep, discussing it with you and no one else beyond the men who rode with you." Vesper turned from the window. "And within hours, men were waiting at a crossing along that exact route, with rope and a net rather than blades, prepared for exactly the kind of taking they attempted. That is not the work of a house watching our movements from a distance and guessing well. That is the work of someone who already knew where you would be standing, before you knew it yourself."

Lucian set the grain report down entirely. "You think someone inside Blackspire told them."

"I think it is the only explanation that does not require Dravenhall's people to be considerably more prescient than any enemy I have faced in thirty years of holding this house together." Vesper's voice carried none of the heat Lucian might have expected from a man naming a betrayal under his own roof — only the same flat, exhausted precision he brought to any problem too large to solve by force. "And I think, if I am honest with you, that I have suspected something of the kind for longer than I have said aloud to anyone, including you."

"Since when."

"Since the intermediary vanished in Firsthold, the season before last." Vesper let that sit a moment, watching Lucian's face for the shape of the memory returning. "At the time, I told you it was a fair cost for pushing the watch as far as we pushed it — that a man that close to being spent by his own house would not have survived being useful much longer regardless of what we did. I still believe that may be true. But I have never fully set aside the other reading of it. That he did not simply grow cautious, or get moved by hands above him. That someone told him to disappear, because someone inside this house's own information chain told Dravenhall's people the watch existed at all."

Lucian sat with that a long moment, the cold, familiar arithmetic of it settling into place the way Dain had trained him to let difficult things settle rather than resisting their shape. "Two threads. A season and a half apart. Both times, information that should only have existed inside rooms like this one reached the one place it could do Dravenhall the most good."

"Yes."

"You did not tell me, the first time."

"No." Vesper did not soften the admission, and did not apologize for it either, which Lucian found, oddly, easier to accept than an apology would have been. "A suspicion without evidence is a poison of its own kind in a house this size, Lucian. Say it aloud too early, to the wrong ears, and you teach a leak to go quiet rather than teaching it to be found. I have spent a season and a half watching, narrowing, saying nothing, because a door that knows it is being watched simply stops opening where you can see it." His expression did not change, but something in his voice did, dropping into the register he used only for the truths he considered genuinely difficult to hand over. "I am telling you now because it stopped being only my burden to carry the moment those men closed on you at that crossing. This is no longer a threat to information. It is a threat to you directly, and you are owed the truth of what we are standing inside of."

"Who could it be."

"That is the honest difficulty of it. Not everyone in this keep knew the parley's route. But enough people did — officers who helped plan the escort, household staff who arrange travel and provisioning, anyone standing close enough to the decisions of this house without being asked to carry the weight of keeping them — that naming a single name from where we stand now would be closer to guessing than knowing. I have narrowed the circle as far as patience allows. I have not narrowed it to one." Vesper's jaw tightened, briefly, the only outward crack in an otherwise carefully held composure. "I dislike very much telling you that this house's own rot may run through people you have known your whole life, without being able to tell you which of them to stop trusting. But I dislike far more the alternative, which is leaving you to walk through these halls believing every face in them is safe, when I no longer believe that myself."

"What do we do."

"We do exactly what you did with the intermediary in Firsthold, only turned back upon ourselves. We do not press for a name directly — a direct search announces itself, and whoever this is will simply go still and wait us out, the way the intermediary's own masters did the moment they suspected a watch. We feed the circle a truth that does not yet exist. A piece of false information, narrow enough that only a handful of people could carry it outward, planted carefully enough that if it reaches Dravenhall's ears in any shape at all, it tells us which door it left through." Vesper's gaze held his, steady, entirely without the gentleness he sometimes allowed himself at the end of a hard conversation. "This is precisely the kind of work I told you, in the cells, that this house has never been permitted the comfort of avoiding. I will not run it without you this time. You have earned a hand in holding this door, not merely being told, afterward, that it was held."

Lucian looked down at his own hands, steady now in a way they had not been steady on the rock shelf, and understood that whatever quiet, unglamorous work waited on the other side of this conversation would ask something different of him than the crossing had — not courage under a blade, but the colder, slower discipline of sitting inside his own home and trusting almost no one in it completely, for as long as it took to learn who deserved that trust and who had already spent it.

"Tell me what you need me to carry," he said, "and to whom."

Something crossed Vesper's face that was neither approval nor comfort, only the particular, weary recognition of a man watching his son step fully into a kind of work he had once hoped, however briefly, that Lucian might have been spared a little longer. "Sit with me, then. This will take the rest of the evening to plan correctly, and I would rather spend the time now than spend it explaining, afterward, why we moved too quickly and lost the one advantage patience still affords us."
