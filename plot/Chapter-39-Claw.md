# Chapter 39: Claw

What was left of the trapper told Lucian more in a single look than every report Greyfen had sent south combined, and he understood, standing over it in the cold shed the outpost used for exactly this kind of grim keeping, why the steward had warned him it would not make for an easy first look at what they were dealing with.

"Bear," said the huntsman Valtor had asked to attend them — an older man named Corbin, weathered the way everyone at Greyfen seemed weathered, missing two fingers on his left hand to some earlier winter's cost. "Not wolf, not anything moving in a pack. The claw spacing tells it plain enough, and so does the bite radius, if you can stomach looking at it long enough to measure." He crouched, unbothered by what lay in front of him in a way that spoke of a man who had done this work too many times to still flinch at it. "What it does not tell plain is the size. I have hunted this stretch of mountain for thirty years, my lord, and taken bear from it more times than I have fingers left to count on. Nothing that size has ever come out of these woods in my lifetime, or my father's, if his own stories were honest."

"An old one, then. Grown far past its natural years."

"Perhaps. Or grown wrong, however that happens, which is not a question I have the learning to answer." Corbin straightened, and something in his weathered face carried more honesty than comfort. "I only know the shape of the thing that did this, and I know it will not be found by riding loud through the treeline hoping to flush it. It will be found by someone who knows where a beast that size dens, and how it feeds, and where it goes to ground when it has eaten its fill and does not wish to be disturbed."

Valtor's gaze moved to Lucian, level and waiting, offering neither the plan nor the objection to it, only the space for Lucian to ask for what the moment actually required.

"Then you will lead the search," Lucian said to Corbin. "Not the Guard. You know this ground and we do not, and I have learned better than to pretend otherwise in front of men whose lives depend on the pretending being false." He turned to Valtor. "We move on Corbin's knowledge, confirmed from the air wherever Ancalion can manage it without driving the animal further than we can follow."

"Then the rest is mine to set," Valtor said.

"Agreed," Lucian said, and meant it as fully as he had ever meant anything.

They spent two days narrowing the ground — Corbin's read of old kill sites and half-frozen game trails, confirmed twice by Ancalion's long, careful passes over ridgelines no horse could safely climb, until the search had shrunk from an entire wilderness to a single steep fold of forest above a frozen stream, where the huntsman swore the animal had denned before and would den again if it had any sense left in it at all. The company moved into position on the third morning under a sky the color of old iron, spreading into a wide, patient arc through the timber the way Valtor had drilled them to, each man given a stretch of ground to hold and a signal to wait for before closing it.

Lucian held the center of the arc, closest to the den's likely mouth, with Riven and Thorne a short distance to either side and Valtor a few lengths back, positioned to see the whole of the encirclement rather than any single piece of it.

The bear broke from the den before the arc had finished closing.

It came out low and fast in a way nothing that size should have been able to manage, a wall of dark fur and muscle that closed the distance to the nearest exposed flank of the line before the men there had fully registered that the waiting was over, and Lucian saw, in the single clear heartbeat before instinct took the decision out of his hands entirely, an opening in its charge that the plan had never asked him to take — a gap in its guard as it turned to meet the flank, its full attention elsewhere, its side bared to anyone close enough and fast enough to reach it before the arc could properly close around it instead.

He took the opening.

It was, he understood even as he moved, the same reckless certainty that had once put a blade across a man's wrist in Firsthold's market square rather than wait for a better angle — not fear, not panic, only the old, familiar conviction that he could end a threat faster alone than by waiting for the shape of a plan to catch up to the danger in front of him. He closed the distance in three strides, blade already rising, and the bear, faster to notice him than he had accounted for, turned from the flank it had been about to maul and met him instead — closer, and far sooner, than the arc's slower approach could yet support.

"Lucian!"

He did not know, in the moment itself, whether the shout was Valtor's or one of his brothers', only that it carried the particular sharp fear of men watching a gap open faster than they could close it. He felt the swipe more than saw it — a blow meant for him that a Guardsman closer to the flank threw himself into instead, driving between Lucian and the animal's claws with a shoulder rather than a blade, and the sound that followed, a heavy, cracking impact of claw against Cold Silver plate, told Lucian before he had time to look that the cost of his three strides had already been paid by someone other than himself.

The bear turned on the fallen man for one terrible instant, and it was that instant, and only that instant, that let Lucian close the last of the distance and drive his blade into the animal's exposed side with the whole of his weight behind it — not the clean, patient work Dain's floor had built in him across a decade, but something closer to raw, furious economy, every wasted motion the plan would have prevented now spent instead on ending the thing as fast as his body would allow. It took three more strikes before the bear went down fully, each one landing exactly where Dain had taught him a strike belonged, and none of them arriving in the order a properly closed arc would have delivered them.

The silence after was worse than the noise had been.

Valtor reached the fallen Guardsman first, kneeling fast in the snow beside him, and the practiced economy of his hands as he checked the man over told Lucian, even from a distance, that the plate had done its work before anyone said as much aloud.

"The armor held," Valtor said, when Lucian reached them, his voice flat in the particular way Lucian had learned meant controlled rather than calm. "No claw found flesh. The shoulder plate is stove in badly enough that the shoulder beneath it is broken, and I would wager two ribs besides, from the way he winces at the breath. Nothing that will not mend." He looked up, and for the first time since Lucian had known him, something in Valtor's pale eyes carried an open, unhidden weight rather than the careful, measured approval he usually rationed out one word at a time. "He will recover fully. It will be some weeks before he holds a blade the way he held one this morning, and longer still before he does it without the memory of today in the joint."

"That is because of me."

"Yes." Valtor did not soften it, and did not need to raise his voice to make it land the way a shout would have landed from anyone else. "You saw an opening the plan had not accounted for, and you took it before the arc could close around what taking it would cost. The animal is dead because of the same three strides that put Dressel on the ground with a broken shoulder. Both of those things are true at once, and you do not get to keep only the one you prefer."

Lucian looked down at the bear's still, enormous shape in the snow, larger even now, dead, than anything Corbin's thirty years had prepared him to expect, and found no argument in himself worth offering. "I should have waited for the arc."

"You should have. You did not, because some part of you still believes that what you can do alone matters more than what the men around you are owed by your patience." Valtor rose, wiping blood that was not his own from his hands with the same unhurried care he brought to everything. "You are not wrong that you ended this faster than the plan would have. You are wrong that ending it faster was worth what Dressel paid so that you could. A commander who cannot tell the difference between those two facts will spend every man under him proving it, one at a time, for as long as he is allowed to keep commanding."

Riven and Thorne had reached them by then, breathing hard, Riven's face carrying an anger that had not yet decided whether it belonged to relief or fear, Thorne's quieter and, in its way, more unsettling for how carefully it was being held in place.

"You could have died," Riven said, low, to Lucian directly, in a voice that was not quite the easy familiarity he usually spoke in. "Dressel has a shattered shoulder because you decided the plan was slower than your own certainty."

"I am aware."

"Say that to him, then. Not to us." Something in Thorne's quiet voice carried more weight than his brother's louder anger had. "He will want to hear it from you directly, once he can hear anything at all."

They carried Dressel back to Greyfen on a hastily lashed litter, the bear's body dragged behind on a second, cruder frame that the outpost's own men insisted on seeing with their own eyes before they would fully believe the thing that had haunted their treeline for a season was actually dead. Greyfen's relief, when it came, was immediate and unguarded — doors opening, faces losing the particular fear they had worn since the column first arrived, the steward gripping Lucian's forearm with both hands and thanking him in a voice that cracked halfway through the words. It did not feel, to Lucian, entirely earned.

He found Dressel that evening, once Greyfen's own healer had finished splinting and binding what needed it and pronounced him sore but sound, and sat beside the narrow cot in the outpost's healing hall for longer than strictly comfortable for either of them.

"I do not require an apology, my lord," Dressel said, wincing as he shifted, his shoulder bound tight and immobile beneath a blanket. "It is the work. I signed for the work knowing what it might cost, and the plate did more for me today than I had any right to expect it would."

"You signed to serve under a command that would not spend you carelessly. I broke that agreement before I fully understood I was making it." Lucian held the man's gaze, and found, saying it, that it cost him more than the fight itself had. "You will recover fully. Valtor has said as much, and I have learned to trust what he says about wounds more than I trust what I feel looking at one. That does not undo what happened. I only wanted you to hear, from me and not from anyone else, that I understand exactly whose choice put you here."

Dressel studied him a moment, something tired and not unkind moving behind his eyes. "Then you have learned something today that most men twice your age never do, my lord, however it was bought. I have served under commanders who would never have sat at this cot at all."

Lucian did not find much comfort in that. He carried the shape of the day home with him regardless — the huntsman's patient knowledge, the arc that had almost held, the three strides he had taken because some old, unbroken certainty in himself still believed speed mattered more than the plan built to make speed unnecessary, and the weeks Dressel's shoulder would need to mend, a debt paid by someone else's body for a decision that had, in the end, still killed the thing it was meant to kill.

Valtor rode beside him for the better part of the return journey without speaking, and it was only as Blackspire's dark shape finally rose against the northern sky that he broke the silence at all.

"You will lead again," the Captain said. "Lord Vesper was right to give you this, and I have not changed my judgment of that simply because today cost more than it should have. But you will carry Dressel's shoulder with you the next time an opening presents itself before your own arc has closed around it, and you will let that memory be heavier than whatever certainty tells you the opening is worth taking alone." He did not look at Lucian as he said it, his gaze fixed instead on the road ahead, the mountain drawing closer with every stride. "That is the whole of what this day was meant to teach you, whether or not either of us would have chosen to teach it this way."

Lucian said nothing further, and rode the rest of the way home carrying the lesson exactly where Valtor had told him to carry it — not as a wound of his own, but as a debt owed to a man who would spend the coming weeks mending from three strides Lucian had taken alone, when the whole of his training had been built to teach him better than to need to.
