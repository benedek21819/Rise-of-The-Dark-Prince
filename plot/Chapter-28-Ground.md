# Chapter 28: Ground

Three days was long enough to learn a city, and not long enough to trust it.

The Caldarion residence sat in the noble quarter above the river, close enough to the palace that its windows caught the white-and-gold towers at every hour of the day, far enough that it kept its own gate, its own walls, its own quiet. Lucian spent the three days the way Dain would have expected him to spend them if Dain had been there to expect anything — walking the grounds until he knew every corner of them, learning the guards' rotations without being told, testing the doors that should have been locked and finding, each time, that they were. The staff were polite and thorough and entirely unsurprised by any of it, which told him something about how long the residence had stood ready for exactly this kind of arrival.

Ancalion did not take well to the roost they gave him — a wide walled yard behind the residence, open to the sky but closed on every side, nothing like the high platform at Blackspire where the whole of the North had always been visible in every direction. He paced it the first night, restless in a way that reached Lucian even through the wall between their rooms, and settled only once Lucian had gone out to sit against his flank in the dark and stayed there long enough for the restlessness to ease into something more like patience. The city beyond the walls did not stop moving even at night. Neither of them was built for that yet.

It was on the third afternoon, from the same high window that had shown him the towers every morning, that Lucian watched the procession finally cross the river bridge below — the black and silver banners smaller from this height than Ancalion's shadow had ever made anything feel, and slower besides, a controlled, deliberate slowness that had nothing to do with the horses' pace and everything to do with the fact that House Caldarion did not arrive anywhere as though it were in a hurry to be let in.

He was waiting at the gate when the carriage stopped, and found that standing there himself, watching for an arrival the way his father had once stood at Blackspire's threshold, felt like something he had not earned the right to yet and did anyway.

Vesper stepped down first, then Riven and Thorne close behind him, both of them scanning the unfamiliar street with the same instinctive, restless alertness before either had fully found his footing on the cobbles — Riven's eyes moving fast and wide, cataloguing exits the way Dain had drilled into all three of them; Thorne's moving slower, quieter, settling on the rooflines and staying there a moment longer than his brother's did, as though he trusted what a place looked like more than what it did in the first instant. It was a small thing. Lucian noticed it anyway, the way he had started noticing small things about his brothers since Firsthold — evidence, however slight, that two boys raised to be nearly interchangeable were not, quite, the same person wearing two faces.

"You look well," Vesper said, which from him was closer to an embrace than most fathers' actual embraces.

"The residence is well kept."

"It has needed to be." Vesper's gaze went past him, briefly, to the walled yard where Ancalion's shape was visible over the rooftop, and something in his father's face eased a fraction that Lucian doubted anyone else would have caught. "You were seen crossing half the kingdom. I trust you understand that every duchy between here and Blackspire is already discussing what they saw."

"That was the intention."

"It was." A pause, the closest thing to satisfaction Vesper allowed himself in public. "Come. The King has asked for us before the evening's formal reception, not after it. He does not want an audience for this conversation, which tells you something about what he intends to discuss."

The palace did not announce itself the way Blackspire did, with cold stone and a mountain's weight behind every hall. It announced itself instead in light — wide windows, pale marble that held the sun long after it should have faded, galleries open on one side to gardens that had never once needed to be defended. Lucian walked it at his father's shoulder and felt, again, the same unfamiliar thing he had felt watching the city rise out of the haze from Ancalion's back: not quite wonder, not quite unease, something that had not yet decided which of the two it wanted to be.

King Aldric received them in a private study rather than the throne room, which Lucian understood, even without being told, as its own kind of statement — the same instinct his father used when deciding which rooms held ceremony and which held truth. The King was older than Vesper by a handful of years and looked it more than the count suggested, grey threading through dark hair, a weariness in his bearing that no amount of crimson and gold quite disguised. He rose when they entered, which Lucian had not expected, and crossed the room to clasp Vesper's forearm in a grip that held longer than protocol required.

"Vesper." Something genuine moved behind the formality. "I am glad to see you standing in this room and not merely hearing that you would be."

"You had reason to doubt it."

"I had a report from Firsthold that took three days to reach me and frightened me for every one of them." Aldric's eyes moved to Lucian then, taking him in fully for what Lucian understood was the first time in several years — not the polite, distant appraisal of a king greeting an ally's heir, but something closer to genuine relief. "And this is the boy who ended it before it could become something I would have had to answer for in front of the whole kingdom."

"Your Majesty."

"You will forgive me if I do not manage the correct formality today." Aldric's mouth curved, brief and tired. "Sit. Both of you. What I have to say is easier said without the throne room's height between us."

They sat. Vesper's posture did not change, but Lucian had learned enough of his father's stillness by now to read the particular quality of this one — attentive in a way that expected the conversation to matter.

"I have read the account you sent," Aldric said, "and I have read between the lines you did not write, which I assume was also your intention." He did not look away from Vesper as he said it. "You believe it was Dravenhall."

"I believe a great many things I cannot prove," Vesper said, "and I did not put a name to any of them in what I sent you, because a name without proof serves no one but whoever wishes to say afterward that House Caldarion accuses its rivals without cause."

"You did not need to write the name. I read Firsthold's account of the operation itself, and I have sat across a council table from men who plan that way for thirty years. I know the shape of it when I see it." Something tightened at the corner of Aldric's mouth. "My wife's brother plans that way."

The silence that followed was not long, but it carried weight enough to fill the room regardless.

"I have not accused Lord Roderic of anything," Vesper said, carefully. "I would not, without proof, even to you."

"No. You are too careful for that, and I am grateful for it, because I am not sure what I would do with an accusation I could neither dismiss nor act on." Aldric rose and crossed to the window, looking out over gardens that gave no indication they had ever known a war. "I married Isolde to end a grievance, Vesper. Four decades, and I still cannot tell you whether it worked or whether it only taught the grievance to wait longer and choose its moments more carefully. If it was Roderic's hand behind Firsthold, he did not act with my wife's knowledge — I believe that as surely as I believe anything in this world — but I have learned not to mistake a wife's innocence for a house's."

"I did not come here to ask you to choose between us," Vesper said. "I came to tell you what happened to my children in your capital's own north, and to ask that the crown's own eyes stay open in whatever direction they can, without requiring you to name a house you cannot yet prove."

"That, I can promise you." Aldric turned back from the window, and for a moment the tiredness in him gave way to something harder, more like the king he had been before decades of careful balancing had worn the edges down. "Lucian."

"Your Majesty."

"You defended your sister with a blade drawn against men who meant to take her, in the middle of a city that had every reason to think itself safe." Aldric held his gaze. "I have three children of my own. I do not know what I would have done in your place at your age, and I suspect I would rather not find out. You have my gratitude, for whatever a king's gratitude is worth against what you actually risked."

"It is worth a great deal, Your Majesty." Lucian said it plainly, and meant it more than the formality suggested. "I would ask only that it be spent on finding what my father cannot yet prove."

Something that might have been approval crossed Aldric's face — quick, and quickly folded back beneath the weariness that seemed to be his default state in this room, as though he had learned long ago that letting anything show for too long cost more than it earned.

"You raised him well," Aldric said to Vesper.

"He raised a good deal of himself," Vesper said. "I only gave him the ground to stand on."

Aldric's gaze moved between them once more, father and son, and something in it settled — not resolved, not comfortable, but settled, the way a scale settles once both sides have finished shifting their weight.

"The reception is in two hours," he said. "Isolde will be there. Dorian, Corvin, Seren — all of them. I would ask that whatever we have said in this room stay in it, for tonight at least. There will be a right time to move against what we suspect. A palace reception, in front of half the noble houses of the kingdom, is not it."

"Understood," Vesper said.

Lucian said nothing, but he understood it too — understood, walking back out through the pale marble galleries with his father's measured stride beside him, that he had just watched two men agree to smile across a room at a woman one of them suspected of harboring, however unknowingly, the family that had tried to kill his sister. It was not a lesson written down anywhere in Dain's training halls. It did not need to be. He was learning it anyway, the way he seemed to learn everything that mattered most in this house — by standing close enough to watch it happen, and being trusted, finally, to understand what he saw.
